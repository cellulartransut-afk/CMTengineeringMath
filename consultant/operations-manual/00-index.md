# Operations Manual

Rev 1 · 2026-11-04

## What this is called and why it matters

What a company runs on is its **operating system**: the written way it does everything, so the work comes out the same every time, whoever is doing it. The main part is the **operations manual**, made of **SOPs (standard operating procedures)**. An SOP is the step-by-step way the company does one recurring job, like pricing a job, hiring a sub or chasing a late payment.

Why it matters: a business that lives only in the owner's head stops when the owner stops. A business with written systems can train people, catch its own mistakes, grow past what one person can do, and one day run without the owner on site. That's the "people and money work for me" stage. Banks, big clients and buyers also trust a business with systems more.

## The five parts of your operating system

| Part | What it is | Where |

## How to use the manual

1. **Follow the SOP**, even when you think you remember it. Pilots use checklists after 10,000 hours for a reason.
2. **When something goes wrong, fix the SOP**, not just the problem. Ask: which step was missing or unclear? Add it. That's how the business learns.
3. **Train people from it.** A new hire reads the SOP, watches you do it once, does it while you watch, then does it alone.
4. **One owner per SOP.** Right now that's you. Later each SOP gets a named process owner (PM, super, office admin) who keeps it current.

## How long these things last before the wording needs updating

For every client and every job you change the **specifics**: names, dates, addresses, scope, prices, payment stages. That's normal and expected. The question is how long the **core wording** holds before it needs rewriting.

| Item | Changes for every client or job | Core wording lasts | Routine review | Update straight away when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contracts (small works, short form, T&M, maintenance, subcontract) | Parties, site, scope, price, payment stages, dates | 3 to 5 years once a Bahamian attorney has reviewed it | Read through yearly. Attorney re-review every 2 to 3 years | A law changes (Contractors Board starts licensing, VAT rate or rules change, a lien law passes, arbitration or procurement rules change); a dispute or near-dispute shows a gap; you move to bigger or different work; you incorporate, register for VAT or hire employees |
| Notice letters, receipts and releases | Names, dates, amounts | Same as the contracts | With the contracts | The contract clause numbers they quote change |
| Proposal / quote template | Scope, price, terms per job | 1 to 2 years | Yearly | You change payment terms, warranty or branding |
| Change order form, daily report | Every use | 3+ years | Yearly | Rarely; only if a gap shows up |
| SOPs | Nothing; you follow them | 1 to 2 years | Every 6 months in year 1, then yearly | A mistake cost money or time; you hire someone; you add a new service; a law or rate changes |
| Checklists | Ticked per job | 1 to 2 years | With the SOPs | A failed inspection, a callback, an injury or a near miss |
| Cost codes | Nothing | 5+ years | Yearly | You start a new kind of work that has no code |
| Unit cost book prices | n/a | Materials 30 to 90 days; sub labour rates 6 to 12 months | Every time you quote | Freight, steel or lumber prices jump; before hurricane season; after any storm |
| Spreadsheet assumptions (VAT, NIB, minimum wage) | Nothing | Until the law changes | January and July. The Bahamas budget comes in late May or June for the fiscal year starting 1 July, and NIB changes have taken effect in July | Any budget or NIB announcement |
| Spreadsheet formulas | New copy per job | Years | When you change how you work | A formula gives a wrong answer |
| Knowledge file 02 (Bahamas rules) | Nothing | 6 to 12 months | Every 6 months: ask the consultant to check it against current sources | News on the Contractors Board, VAT, NIB, building code or procurement |
| Business licence, insurance, BCA membership | n/a | 1 year | At each annual renewal | You change what work you do or how big it is |

**Rule of thumb:** contract wording reviewed by an attorney can carry you through Stage 1 and most of Stage 2, so roughly your first 2 to 5 years, with small edits. What goes stale fastest is **prices**, then **rates and laws**, then **procedures**, and slowest of all the **contract wording**.
