# Part A. The seven checklists in `templates/checklists.md`

## Checklist 1. Vet a new client before you quote

**Why this checklist exists.** A quote costs you hours: site visit, takeoff, sub quotes, writing it up. A bad client costs more than no client. The Bahamas has no lien law (you cannot put a legal claim on the house for unpaid work), so if a client won't pay, your only route is the courts. The cheapest protection is not signing the wrong client. These nine questions take about 15 minutes.

### 1.1 Who owns the property?

**What it means.** Find out who legally owns the land and building. For new builds, ask to see the conveyance (the legal document that transferred the land to them) or the survey plan.

**Why it is here.** Only the owner, or someone with the owner's written authority, can give you the right to work there, and the owner is the person you hold to pay. Gone wrong: a nephew hires you to fix his aunt's house while she lives in Florida. Halfway through she says she never agreed and won't pay. He has no money. You can't put a claim on the house. On family land shared by several relatives, another relative can stop the job.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask early and plainly: "Before I price this, who owns the property? I need the owner's full legal name for the contract."
2. New build or addition: "Can I see the conveyance or survey? I need it to check setbacks and set out the building."
3. Check the name on the document is the person who will sign your contract. Photograph or copy it for the job file.
4. If someone else will sign (a relative, tenant or property manager), ask for a short letter signed by the owner giving them authority to contract and pay.
5. If it is family land or ownership is unclear, ask the client to confirm with their attorney before you go further.

**Done looks like.** The owner's full legal name is in your job file. It matches the person signing, or you hold a signed letter of authority. For new work, a copy of the conveyance or survey is on file.

### 1.2 Who decides and who pays? Same person?

**What it means.** The decider chooses scope and finishes. The payer funds the job. They are often different people: a couple, an owner abroad with a relative on island, a company manager and its accounts office, a bank loan.

**Why it is here.** If the decider is not the payer, you get approvals the payer won't honour. Gone wrong: the wife approves an upgraded tile change worth B$1,200 on WhatsApp. The husband, who pays, refuses: "I never agreed to that." The tile is already down.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "Who will make decisions on site, and who will pay the invoices?"
2. If they are different people, name the payer as the Owner in the contract. Name the decider as the Owner's representative, with written authority to approve selections and change orders, or require both to sign change orders.
3. If a bank loan funds the job, ask how and when the bank releases money. Banks often pay in stages after their own inspection. Match your payment schedule to that, and confirm the deposit comes from the client's own cash.
4. Owner abroad: agree that email and WhatsApp count for notices and approvals. Your contract template allows it (clause 17.3).

**Done looks like.** Decider and payer are named in the job file and the contract. Everyone knows who can sign a change order.

### 1.3 Budget stated, and realistic for the scope?

**What it means.** The client tells you what they plan to spend, and you judge whether it matches what they want built.

**Why it is here.** A client who wants a B$40,000 kitchen for B$15,000 will either not sign after you spent 10 hours pricing, or sign and fight every invoice. Gone wrong: you spend two evenings on takeoff and sub quotes. The client says "my cousin said B$8,000" and disappears.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "What budget have you set aside for this?"
2. If they won't say, give a range from real jobs: "Bathrooms like this usually come in between B$[ ] and B$[ ]. Is that in your range?" Until you have your own job history, ask a contractor you trust for ranges. For scale, the example bathroom in `01-roadmap.md` priced at about B$12,000.
3. If their number is far below, say so now and offer options: smaller scope, cheaper finishes, do it in phases.
4. Don't do a full estimate for someone whose budget can't reach the job.

**Done looks like.** The budget is written in your job file, and before you priced it you told the client whether it fits.

### 1.4 How did they find you? Referral beats cold.

**What it means.** The source of the lead: a referral from someone you know, a past client, an agent, social media, your sign on a job.

**Why it is here.** A referred client arrives with some trust and has a reputation to protect with the person who sent them. A cold lead is more likely to be shopping five contractors on price alone. It is not a rule, it is a weight on the scale. It also tells you which marketing works. Gone wrong: you chase every Facebook enquiry and spend 30 hours a month quoting people who never sign, while referral leads wait days for a call back.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "How did you hear about us?"
2. If referred, call the person who referred them: "Thanks for sending them. Anything I should know?"
3. Log the source in your lead list (see `consultant/tools/office-tracker.xlsx` and [`05-spreadsheets-explained.md`](05-spreadsheets-explained.md)).

**Done looks like.** Source recorded. After 20 leads you can see which sources turn into paid jobs.

### 1.5 Have they fired other contractors on this job? Ask why.

**What it means.** Find out whether anyone else has already worked on this job and left, and why. One is normal. Two is a warning.

**Why it is here.** The market has plenty of contractors who don't finish, so one firing can be the contractor's fault. Two or more usually means the client is the problem: slow pay, constant changes, impossible standards. Also, taking over someone else's half-done work means inheriting their mistakes. Gone wrong: you take over a half-built addition. The tie beam has no hurricane straps. The client expects you to "just carry on" at your price, and to fix the last man's mistakes for free.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask straight: "Has anyone else worked on this? What happened?"
2. Look for clues on site: half-finished work, materials nobody explains.
3. If someone was fired, ask: "Is there any dispute or money still owed to them? Who owns the materials on site?" Don't step into an open fight.
4. If you take over existing work: photograph all of it before you start, write in your quote that you do not warrant work done by others, and price any fixes as separate items.

**Done looks like.** The history is written down. Photos of existing work are on file. Your quote excludes previous work. You made a conscious decision to go ahead or walk.

### 1.6 Will they pay a 30% to 40% deposit? If not, walk.

**What it means.** The deposit is money paid on signing, before you buy anything. It must cover the materials you buy first plus mobilisation (the cost of getting set up on site).

**Why it is here.** With no lien law, the deposit is your only security at the start. Without it, you finance the client's job with your own money. Gone wrong: B$10,000 job, no deposit. You buy B$3,500 of materials on your trade account. The client "has a delay at the bank". Your money sits in their house for six weeks and your supplier wants paying in 30 days.

**How to do it.**
1. Say it before you price, not after: "Our terms are a 40% deposit on signing, then stage payments as the work is done. Does that work for you?"
2. Size the deposit with real numbers. Add the materials you must order in the first two weeks plus mobilisation. Example: materials B$3,500 + setup B$500 = B$4,000 on a B$10,000 job = 40%.
3. Jobs heavy in materials (cabinets, impact windows) need a bigger deposit, or a separate payment when that order is placed.
4. Don't order anything until the money has cleared in your business account. A cheque "in the post" is not a deposit.

**Done looks like.** The deposit is in the quote and the contract payment schedule. Your bank shows cleared funds before the first purchase order goes out.

### 1.7 Do they need a permit and drawings, and do they have them?

**What it means.** A building permit from Building Control is required for new construction, additions and alterations, including decks, sheds, retaining walls and fences. Anything structural needs drawings by a qualified architect or engineer.

**Why it is here.** Work without a permit can be stopped, and the client may not get a certificate of occupancy or a power connection. Permits take time; an older benchmark put Building Control at about 120 days. Gone wrong: you start a carport with no permit. A stop notice arrives. Your crew sits idle, the client blames you, and the posts you set may have to come out.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "Do you have drawings and a building permit for this?"
2. If the work looks cosmetic (painting, like-for-like tile), ask Building Control whether a permit is needed and get the answer in writing. An email reply is fine.
3. If a permit is needed and the client doesn't have one, choose: the client gets drawings and permit (and you exclude both from your price), or you offer them as a separate scope using a registered architect or engineer.
4. In the quote and contract, make the start date "[ ] days after the permit is issued".

**Done looks like.** A permit number in the file, or written confirmation that none is needed, or a dated plan for who gets it with the contract start tied to it.

### 1.8 Timeline realistic?

**What it means.** Compare the date they want with the time the job really takes.

**Why it is here.** Clients often have a fixed date: Christmas, a tenant moving in, family arriving from abroad. If impact windows take 8 to 16 weeks, a 6-week promise is a lie. Late jobs make angry clients and sometimes delay damages. Gone wrong: you promise a rental make-ready by the 1st. The AC unit has a 4-week lead time. The landlord loses a month's rent and blames you.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "When do you need it finished, and what happens on that date?"
2. Check the real time: permit, lead times (table in `04-estimating-and-materials.md`), hurricane season, your subs' availability, Family Island freight.
3. Tell them the honest timeline with the reasons. If not everything fits, offer what can be done by their date.
4. Put the duration, and what extends it, in the contract.

**Done looks like.** A duration you believe, with long-lead items named, agreed in writing.

### 1.9 Gut check

**What it means.** Your judgement of how the client behaves before you sign. If they argue price hard before you start, they argue payment harder at the end.

**Why it is here.** Behaviour before signing is the best behaviour you will ever see from a client. Gone wrong: a client beats you down 15% "or I'll use someone else", then disputes every stage payment. You work the last month for nothing.

**How to do it.** Watch for these flags:
- Pushes you to drop the deposit, or to start before signing.
- Asks for a "cash price" with no paperwork to dodge VAT or business licence tax. Say no. That is not how you work, and it puts you on the wrong side of the law.
- Won't sign a contract: "we're family", "my word is good".
- Rude to your workers, or lies about small things.
- Changes the scope every conversation.

One flag: be careful and tighten the contract. Two or more: walk away. The jobs you don't take can't hurt you.

**Done looks like.** A conscious yes or no, with the reason written in your lead notes.

## Checklist 2. Vet a new subcontractor

**Why this checklist exists.** As a paper contractor, your subs' work is your product. You carry the liability for their quality, safety and legality even though you never touch a tool. A bad sub costs more than a high price.

### 2.1 Visit a job they are working on now. Look at the quality and the site.

**What it means.** Go and see their current work in person before you hire them.

**Why it is here.** Phone photos can be anyone's work. A live site shows how they really work: quality, tidiness, crew size, whether the boss shows up. Gone wrong: a tile setter shows beautiful Instagram photos. On your job the tiles sit uneven (lippage) and sound hollow when tapped. You pay to rip out and redo.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "Where are you working this week? I'd like to come and see the work."
2. Go early in the morning. Are they there on time? How many people?
3. Look at their trade. Block: straight lines, full joints, plumb walls. Tile: flat, even joints, no hollow sound when you tap with a coin. Paint: clean lines at edges, no drips or runs. Electrical: neat runs, boxes fixed firm.
4. Look at the site: clean and organised, PPE worn, ladders and scaffold safe.
5. If they won't let you see current work, treat that as a no.

**Done looks like.** Dated notes and photos of their current job in `my-business/subs.md`.

### 2.2 Two references from contractors, called.

**What it means.** Get the names of two contractors they worked for recently, and phone them yourself.

**Why it is here.** A contractor judges on what matters to you: on time, quality, honest billing, finishing. A homeowner reference mostly tells you the sub was friendly. Gone wrong: you skip references. The plumber walks off halfway through your job. Later you learn he did the same to two contractors last year.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask the sub: "Give me two contractors you worked for in the last 12 months."
2. Call them. Ask: "What did they do for you? Were they on time? Did they finish? Any callbacks? Any surprise extras on the bill? Would you hire them again?"
3. The last question matters most. Listen for a pause.

**Done looks like.** Two calls logged: date, who you spoke to, what they said.

### 2.3 Business licence, NIB letter of good standing, public liability certificate.

**What it means.** Three documents.
- **Business licence:** they are a legal business.
- **NIB letter of good standing:** they are paid up on their workers' National Insurance, so an injured worker is covered by NIB industrial benefits.
- **Public liability certificate naming you:** their insurer pays for injury or damage their work causes to others, and protects you as an additional insured.

**Why it is here.** An uninsured sub's injured worker becomes your problem. Gone wrong: the sub's labourer falls off a ladder and breaks his arm. The sub never paid NIB. The worker's family comes to you and to the client.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask before they start. Your subcontract clause 4 makes it a condition: "Before we start I need copies of your business licence, your NIB letter of good standing, and an insurance certificate naming my company as additional insured."
2. Check each one: the name matches the sub's legal name on your subcontract; the dates are current; the insurance limit is at least the amount in your subcontract; your company is named.
3. Ask the sub's insurance broker to email the certificate straight to you. It is harder to fake.
4. Write the expiry dates in `subs.md` and in your phone calendar.

**Done looks like.** Three copies on file, "yes" in the `subs.md` document columns, expiry dates in your calendar. No documents, no start.

### 2.4 Trade licence (electrical, plumbing).

**What it means.** Electricians and plumbers must be licensed for their trades, and their work is inspected.

**Why it is here.** Unlicensed electrical or plumbing work fails inspection. No passed inspections, no certificate of occupancy, no final payment. Bad wiring burns houses. Gone wrong: you use a cheap "electrician" with no licence. The inspector refuses the rough-in. You pay a licensed electrician to redo it and lose two weeks.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask to see the licence. Photograph it.
2. Ask Building Control how to confirm that a licence is current and what class of work it covers. Ask the electrician or plumber the same question.
3. Make sure the licensed person is the one responsible for your job, not a name lent to somebody else.

**Done looks like.** A copy of a current licence on file, matched to the person who will do and sign for the work.

### 2.5 Crew legally allowed to work in The Bahamas.

**What it means.** Every worker the sub brings is a Bahamian citizen, a permanent resident, or holds a valid work permit or other permission to work.

**Why it is here.** Employing someone without permission is an offence. It happens on your site, under your name. An immigration check stops the job, and the story travels. Gone wrong: the drywall sub brings four new men on a Monday. Two have no permits. Immigration visits. The job stops and the client hears about it from a neighbour.

**How to do it.**
1. Before they start, get a crew list: name and status (citizen, resident, permit holder), with a copy of the permit for any permit holder. Your subcontract clause 4(d) requires it.
2. Have the sub sign that every person is legally allowed to work.
3. Spot-check. Compare the people on site with the list. A new face goes on the list before they work.
4. If you are unsure what a permit allows, ask the Department of Immigration.
5. A breach of this clause lets you end the subcontract immediately (clause 15.1).

**Done looks like.** A signed crew list on file, updated whenever the crew changes, with spot checks noted in your daily reports.

### 2.6 Own tools and transport.

**What it means.** The sub brings their own tools and gets their crew to site without you.

**Why it is here.** Two reasons. The law: if you supply the tools, control their hours and pay by the hour, they are probably your employee, not a sub, and NIB applies to you. Reliability: a sub who needs a ride and borrows your tools will be late and will wear out your kit. Gone wrong: you pick up the "sub" every morning and lend him your mixer. He gets hurt. He looks like your employee, and you never registered him with NIB.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask: "What tools and equipment do you bring? How does your crew get to site?"
2. Check it on the job visit (2.1).
3. If they need your tools and transport, they are labour, not a sub. Either hire them properly as employees (register with NIB first) or don't use them.

**Done looks like.** Subcontract clause 1.3 lists what they supply, and it matches what you see on site.

### 2.7 Quote in writing for a defined scope.

**What it means.** A written, dated price from the sub for an exact scope that you gave them.

**Why it is here.** "I'll do the bathroom for B$2,500" means nothing. Does it include removing the old tile? Waterproofing? Grout? Later the sub says no and wants more. And you can't level quotes you can't compare. Gone wrong: the painter's spoken price "for the house" turns out not to include ceilings or exterior trim. B$900 extra, or a half-painted house.

**How to do it.**
1. Send each sub the same scope: drawings plus a written list of items, what you supply, what they supply.
2. Ask for a written quote back: price, inclusions, exclusions, duration, crew size, validity date.
3. Level the quotes side by side (see **bid leveling** in the glossary).
4. A detailed WhatsApp message is acceptable. A quote on their letterhead with their business licence number is better.

**Done looks like.** Written, dated quotes that match your scope sheet, leveled against each other.

### 2.8 Will they sign your subcontract? If not, don't use them.

**What it means.** The sub signs your subcontract agreement (`consultant/templates/subcontract-agreement.md`) before they start.

**Why it is here.** The subcontract is where your control lives: scope, payment timing, 10% retention, flow-down, insurance, backcharges, no direct dealing with your client, warranty, termination. Without it you have a handshake. A sub who refuses usually wants to be free to walk off, add extras, or deal with your client behind your back. Gone wrong: no subcontract. The roofer finishes 80%, then demands B$1,500 more to "finish properly". You have nothing in writing to hold him to.

**How to do it.**
1. Send the subcontract with their quote attached as the scope.
2. Walk them through the key clauses in person: when they get paid, retention, backcharges, no direct dealing, cleanup, safety.
3. Accept reasonable changes. Send anything unusual to your attorney. See also `consultant/templates/contract-guide.md`.
4. No signature, no start. No exceptions for friends or family.

**Done looks like.** A signed subcontract in the job file before they set foot on site.

### 2.9 Start them on a small job first.

**What it means.** Give a new sub a small, low-risk scope before you trust them with a big one.

**Why it is here.** A bad sub on a B$1,500 job costs you a day. On a B$40,000 job it costs you the client. Gone wrong: a new block crew gets your first addition. The walls are an inch out of plumb. Two courses come down and get relaid at your cost.

**How to do it.**
1. Give a small, separate scope: hang a door, patch a wall, paint one room.
2. Inspect closely. Rate them 1 to 5 using the guide in `subs.md` (5: shows up, right first time, clean site, honest billing; 3: needs watching; 1: never again).
3. Only a 4 or 5 gets the bigger job.

**Done looks like.** A rating and notes in `subs.md` after their first job with you.

## Checklist 3. Before you send a quote

**Why this checklist exists.** The job is won or lost in the estimate and the contract, not on site. Most bad estimates fail by missing items, not by wrong prices. This list catches missing items and checks that the price actually makes money.

### 3.1 Site visited and photographed

**What it means.** You walk the site yourself, measure it, and photograph everything before you price.

**Why it is here.** Drawings don't show the narrow lane the concrete truck can't get down, the rotten fascia, the leaning boundary wall, or the neighbour who complains about noise. Photos of existing damage protect you when the client later says you caused it. Gone wrong: you price a bathroom from the client's phone photos. On day one you find the only access is through the client's bedroom and the subfloor is rotten. Two unpriced problems.

**How to do it.**
1. Bring a tape measure (and a laser measure if you have one), your phone, a notebook, a torch and a level.
2. Check access: can a truck get in? Where will the materials, skip bin and toilet go? Where do people park?
3. Find power and water you can use. Look at the electrical panel and find the water shut-off.
4. Look for existing damage: cracks, stains, soft floors, rust stains on concrete, termite trails, sagging roof lines. Photograph each one.
5. Photograph every room or side of the building in the work area. Make sure photos carry the date.
6. Ask the client: "Any leaks, termites or past repairs I should know about?"

**Done looks like.** A dated photo folder, a measured sketch, your notes, and a list of questions for the client.

### 3.2 Every work item in sequence listed

**What it means.** A list of every task, written in the order it will be built.

**Why it is here.** Following the build order makes gaps obvious: you notice nothing sits between "remove tile" and "new tile". Gone wrong: you price tile but forget waterproofing and the flood test. That is about B$350 of material and a day of labour out of your profit.

**How to do it.**
1. Write tasks in the construction order from `04-estimating-and-materials.md`: demolition, foundation, walls, roof, openings, MEP, finishes, cleanup.
2. For bigger jobs, use the 26-step sequence in [`03-how-a-house-gets-built.md`](03-how-a-house-gets-built.md) as your checklist.
3. Example, bathroom: protect floors and furniture; remove fixtures; demolish tile; haul debris; inspect walls and floor; plumbing changes; electrical changes (GFCI outlet, fan); new board; waterproofing; flood test; floor tile; wall tile; grout and seal; vanity and top; toilet and fixtures; accessories; paint; clean.
4. Ask a sub you trust to read your list: "What did I miss?"

**Done looks like.** A numbered list where every step from start to a clean handover appears.

### 3.3 Quantities with math shown

**What it means.** Every quantity (square feet, blocks, cubic yards) is written with the calculation that produced it.

**Why it is here.** If you can see the math, you and others can check it. Mistakes hide in numbers with no working. Gone wrong: you write "wall tile 90 sq ft" from memory. The real area is 155 sq ft. You run out halfway, and the second batch doesn't match the first.

**How to do it.**
1. Write the formula next to each item. Wall tile: walls (5 + 5 + 8) ft x 8 ft high = 144 sq ft, less window 2 x 3 = 6 sq ft, = 138 sq ft, + 12% waste = 155 sq ft.
2. Use the rules of thumb in `04-estimating-and-materials.md`: concrete cubic feet / 27, blocks 1.125 per sq ft, paint coverage per gallon, drywall area / 32.
3. Use the estimate sheet in `consultant/tools/estimator.xlsx` so the math is saved with the job.

**Done looks like.** Every quantity in the estimate shows working that someone else could check.

### 3.4 Materials priced landed and delivered, quotes dated

**What it means.** Each material price is what it costs you delivered to site (including freight, duty, VAT, broker, trucking and breakage), taken from a dated quote.

**Why it is here.** The shelf price is not your cost. Prices move, so an old price is a wrong price. Gone wrong: you price impact doors from a Florida website at B$8,000. Landed and delivered they cost about B$11,600. You lose B$3,600.

**How to do it.**
1. Local materials: a written quote from the yard with its date, validity and delivery charge.
2. Imports: a Florida quote, run through the Landed Cost sheet in `estimator.xlsx`. Ask a licensed customs broker for the duty rate on each item.
3. Write the supplier, quote number and date next to each line.
4. Ask the client whether they have a duty concession.

**Done looks like.** Every material line has a source, a date and a delivered price.

### 3.5 Sub quotes in writing

**What it means.** Every trade in your estimate is priced from a written sub quote, not a guess.

**Why it is here.** For a paper contractor, sub prices are most of the cost. A guessed trade price is a guess at most of your job. Gone wrong: you guess the plumber at B$1,500. His real quote after you win is B$2,400.

**How to do it.**
1. Send the same scope to two subs per trade (see 2.7).
2. Level the quotes.
3. Check their validity date covers your quote's validity.
4. Use the leveled price in the estimate and keep the quote in the job file.

**Done looks like.** A written, leveled quote behind every trade line.

### 3.6 General conditions added

**What it means.** The costs of running the job that belong to no single trade. Full list in `04-estimating-and-materials.md`: supervision time, travel and fuel, temporary toilet, skip bins and dump fees, temporary water and power, scaffolding, small tools and consumables, PPE and first aid, permits if included, job insurance, security, final clean, protection of existing finishes, hurricane prep (June to November), photos and documents.

**Why it is here.** These are real costs that no sub quote includes. Gone wrong: a six-week bathroom job. You forget two skip swaps, your fuel driving to site every day, and floor protection. About B$700 disappears from a B$2,600 gross profit.

**How to do it.**
1. Go down the general conditions list line by line on every job.
2. Price each line or mark it "n/a", so you know you thought about it.
3. Price your own site time. Even if your salary sits in overhead, the hours on this job are real.

**Done looks like.** A general conditions section in the estimate with every line priced or marked n/a.

### 3.7 Family Island logistics priced (if applicable)

**What it means.** Separate cost lines for everything extra on a Family Island job: freight both ways for materials and equipment, crew flights or ferry, housing, food, per diem, local vehicle, weather and boat delays, double handling, breakage, extra supervision trips.

**Why it is here.** Family Island work costs more, and a percentage guess hides how much. Gone wrong: you price an Exuma repair like a Nassau job. Crew housing, the mailboat and a three-day weather delay eat the whole profit.

**How to do it.**
1. Get freight quotes from the mailboat or barge operator, both ways.
2. Price crew travel, housing, food and per diem: per person, per day, times the days.
3. Add days for weather and boat delays to both the schedule and the cost.
4. Add breakage for double handling: 2% to 5%, more for fragile items.
5. If the numbers don't work at a price the client accepts, don't take the job.

**Done looks like.** A Family Island section in the estimate, built from real quotes, not a percentage.

### 3.8 Contingency added

**What it means.** A line for unknowns: 3% to 5% for clear scope, 10% to 15% for renovation with unknowns.

**Why it is here.** Something small always goes wrong: an extra bag of thinset, a cracked tile batch, an extra skip. Contingency covers your own small problems. Hidden damage the client owns (rot, termites) is a change order, not contingency. Gone wrong: zero contingency on a renovation. Three small surprises take B$900 out of profit.

**How to do it.** Multiply direct cost before contingency by the right percentage. Bathroom example from `01-roadmap.md`: B$8,900 x 5% = B$445.

**Done looks like.** A contingency line, at a rate that matches how much of the job you can't see.

### 3.9 Margin checked (not markup confused for margin)

**What it means.** You price to the margin you need using price = cost / (1 - margin), then check it.

**Why it is here.** Markup is added to cost. Margin is the share of the price you keep. Mix them up and you underprice every job. Gone wrong: cost B$9,345, target 22% margin. You add 22% markup: 9,345 x 1.22 = B$11,401. Real margin: 2,056 / 11,401 = 18%. The right price is 9,345 / 0.78 = B$11,981. You gave away B$580.

**How to do it.**
1. Know your target margin: overhead % + profit % (`06-money.md`). Small jobs: 30% to 40%.
2. Price = direct cost / (1 - target margin).
3. Check: (price - cost) / price = your target.
4. Use the markup and margin sheet in `estimator.xlsx`.

**Done looks like.** The margin, worked out on the price, equals or beats your target.

### 3.10 Exclusions listed

**What it means.** A written list in the quote of what the price does not include.

**Why it is here.** Anything you don't exclude, the client assumes is included. Gone wrong: you renovate a kitchen. The client assumes you move the fridge, pay the BPL reconnection and repaint the hallway. None of it priced. You argue or you pay.

**How to do it.**
1. Start from the standard exclusions list in `03-contracts.md`.
2. Always include: permit fees unless stated, hidden damage found after opening up, utility connection fees, moving contents.
3. Delete the lines that don't apply. Add job-specific ones.

**Done looks like.** A "Not included" section in the quote, written for this job.

### 3.11 Payment schedule keeps your cash positive

**What it means.** At every payment stage, the money you have received is more than the money you have spent.

**Why it is here.** If cash goes negative, you are financing the client. Gone wrong: a B$40,000 job with a 20% deposit and the next payment at "50% complete". You spend B$20,000 before the second payment, with only B$8,000 in. You are B$12,000 short and the supplier is calling.

**How to do it.**
1. List what you will spend by each milestone.
2. Set the deposit and stages so money in stays ahead. Example from `06-money.md`, B$40,000 job:

| Milestone | Client pays B$ | In to date B$ | Spent to date B$ | Cash B$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signing, 40% | 16,000 | 16,000 | 12,000 | +4,000 |
| Demolition and rough-in, 25% | 10,000 | 26,000 | 20,000 | +6,000 |
| Finishes installed, 25% | 10,000 | 36,000 | 29,000 | +7,000 |
| Completion, 10% | 4,000 | 40,000 | 31,000 | +9,000 |

3. Test it in the Cash Flow sheet of `estimator.xlsx`.
4. If any row goes negative, change the schedule before the quote goes out.

**Done looks like.** Every row of the cash test is positive.

### 3.12 Validity date matches supplier quote validity

**What it means.** Your quote is valid for no longer than the supplier and sub quotes behind it.

**Why it is here.** If your quote lasts 30 days but the lumber price lasts 7, the client can sign on day 25 at your old price while your cost has gone up. Gone wrong: the client signs on day 28. Lumber is up 8%. B$480 lost on a B$6,000 lumber package.

**How to do it.**
1. Check the validity of every supplier and sub quote.
2. Set your validity to the shortest one, or ask suppliers to hold their price for your period.
3. For long jobs or volatile items (steel, lumber, roofing, freight), add the escalation clause (template clause 9).

**Done looks like.** Your quote's "valid for" date falls on or before every supplier and sub validity date, or the escalation clause covers the gap.

### 3.13 Cost per sq ft or per room compared with past jobs

**What it means.** A sanity check. Divide the price by floor area or by number of rooms and compare with your past jobs.

**Why it is here.** If this bathroom comes in at half your usual price, you missed something. If it is double, you counted something twice or the scope is unusual. Gone wrong: a quote goes out 30% low because the tile was entered per box instead of per square foot. Nobody checked.

**How to do it.**
1. Divide price by floor area, or per room for bathrooms and kitchens.
2. Compare with your `jobs.md` log and cost book. Until you have about 10 jobs of your own, ask an experienced contractor whether the number looks right.
3. If it is far off, find out why before you send it.

**Done looks like.** A one-line note on the estimate: "This job B$[ ] per sq ft; past average B$[ ]. Difference explained by [ ]."

## Checklist 4. Before any concrete pour

**Why this checklist exists.** Concrete is the one thing you can't take back. Once it is poured, a mistake is permanent or costs a jackhammer. Footings, slab and tie beam are all structural. Run this list for every pour, every time, even small ones.

### 4.1 Inspection passed (yours and Building Control where required)

**What it means.** Two inspections before concrete. First your own hold point check. Then Building Control's stage inspection, where the permit requires one (footings, slab, tie beam).

**Why it is here.** Pouring before the inspection can mean the concrete is ordered broken out, or later stages refused. Gone wrong: the ready-mix truck is booked, the inspector is running late, the crew says "just pour, it's fine". Building Control won't pass the next stage without proof of the steel. You pay to cut into the concrete to show it.

**How to do it.**
1. Do your own check first (H1, H4 or H5 in Part D) with the drawing in your hand. Fix everything you find.
2. Book Building Control. Ask the office how inspections are booked and how much notice they need. Book early.
3. Be on site when the inspector comes. Walk with them. Write down anything they want changed, and fix it.
4. Ask what record you get of a pass (a signed card, a notice, an entry in their system) and keep it.
5. Book the concrete only once you know the inspection date. Better: a provisional booking you can move.

**Done looks like.** Your daily report records your check and Building Control's pass, with the time, the inspector's name and the result, and you hold the office's record of the pass.

### 4.2 Rebar size, spacing, laps, cover on chairs checked against the drawing

**What it means.** Check the steel: right bar size, right spacing, overlaps (laps) long enough, and the bars sitting up on chairs so concrete will cover them all round.

**Why it is here.** Concrete without the right steel in the right place is weak, and once it is poured nobody can see the mistake until it fails. Gone wrong: the crew uses #4 bars where the drawing says #5 (about a third less steel), laps them 12 inches instead of 30, and lays the cage on the dirt. All of it buried.

**How to do it.**
1. Hold the structural drawing. Read the bar size, spacing, lap length and cover for this pour.
2. Bar size: check the size marked on the bar against the drawing.
3. Spacing: tape-measure between bars at several places, not just one.
4. Laps: measure each overlap. Example: 48 bar diameters of a #5 bar = 48 x 5/8 inch = 30 inches.
5. Cover: measure from bar to formwork or ground. Footings: 3 inches. Other members: per the drawing. Bars on chairs or dobies, never on stones, broken block or soil.
6. Ties tight; the cage doesn't shift when you push it.
7. Photograph each area with a tape measure in the shot.

**Done looks like.** Every dimension checked against the drawing, every defect fixed, photos taken and filed.

### 4.3 Straps, anchors, sleeves, conduits set

**What it means.** Everything that must be cast into the concrete is in place before the pour: hurricane straps (tie beam), anchor bolts, sleeves for pipes, electrical conduits.

**Why it is here.** Adding them after the pour means drilling or chopping concrete, and retrofitted straps are weaker than cast-in ones. Gone wrong: the tie beam is poured with straps missing at four truss positions. The fix is drilled-in anchors: cost, delay, and a weaker roof connection right where the wind will pull.

**How to do it.**
1. Get the truss layout drawing and mark every truss position on the formwork.
2. Check there is a strap at every mark, of the type on the drawing, set to the depth the drawing or manufacturer requires, tied so it can't move during the pour.
3. Count straps. Count trusses. The numbers must match.
4. Walk the pour with the plumber and electrician: "Is every sleeve and conduit you need in this pour in place?" Get each to confirm.

**Done looks like.** Strap count matches truss count, the plumber and electrician have confirmed in writing (a WhatsApp is fine), photos taken.

### 4.4 Formwork braced and clean, oiled

**What it means.** The forms are strong and braced so they can't move or burst, clean inside, and coated with release agent so they come off clean.

**Why it is here.** Wet concrete weighs roughly 4,000 lb per cubic yard. Weak forms bulge or blow out. Debris left inside (wood offcuts, cups, leaves) gets cast in and makes weak spots. Gone wrong: a tie beam form bursts mid-pour. Concrete on the ground, a crooked beam, half a day of cleanup, and a weak joint where the pour restarted.

**How to do it.**
1. Push hard on the forms. They shouldn't move. Look for braces at regular spacing and at every corner.
2. Check the tops are at the right level with a level or laser.
3. Look inside. Pick or blow out all debris.
4. Oil goes on before the steel goes in. Oil on the rebar weakens the grip between steel and concrete.
5. Close gaps that would leak cement paste.

**Done looks like.** Forms rigid, level, clean and oiled along their full length, and you have walked every foot of it.

### 4.5 Concrete ordered: strength, slump, quantity + 5% to 10%, time

**What it means.** The order states the strength the drawing requires, the slump, the volume plus 5% to 10% extra, and the arrival time.

**Why it is here.** Wrong strength means a weaker building than designed. Too little concrete means a "cold joint" (a weak seam where fresh concrete meets concrete that has already started to set) while you wait for another truck. Gone wrong: you order exactly 8.0 cubic yards for an 8.0 yard slab. The ground is uneven, you are half a yard short, and the next truck takes 90 minutes.

**How to do it.**
1. Read the strength and any mix requirements from the drawings or specs.
2. Calculate volume: length x width x depth in feet / 27. Example: slab 30 x 40 ft x 4 inches = 30 x 40 x 0.333 = 400 cu ft. 400 / 27 = 14.8 cu yd. Add 7% = 15.9. Order 16 cu yd.
3. Call the supplier with: strength, slump per spec, volume, site address, start time, chute or pump, number of trucks and the gap between them.
4. Get the order confirmed in writing (WhatsApp or email).
5. When each truck arrives, check the delivery ticket against your order before it pours.

**Done looks like.** A written order confirmation that matches the drawing, and every delivery ticket checked and kept.

### 4.6 Pump or access confirmed

**What it means.** The truck can reach the place the concrete goes, or a concrete pump is booked.

**Why it is here.** If the truck can't reach, you end up wheelbarrowing 16 yards in the sun, the concrete stiffens, and somebody adds water. Gone wrong: the truck can't get past the neighbour's wall. No pump booked. Part of the load starts to set in the drum.

**How to do it.**
1. Walk the route the truck will take: width, overhead wires, soft ground, slope, turning space.
2. Measure from where the truck can stand to the farthest point of the pour. Ask the supplier how far their chute reaches.
3. If it won't reach, book a pump through the supplier or a pump operator and confirm the time.
4. Tell the neighbours about the truck and any blocked access.

**Done looks like.** Route checked, pump booked if needed, times confirmed in writing.

### 4.7 Vibrator, tools, labour, water for curing, cover in case of rain

**What it means.** Everything for placing and protecting the concrete is on site before the first truck: vibrator (with a backup or a rod for rodding), shovels, rakes, screed boards, floats, trowels, enough people, water and burlap or plastic for curing, and tarps in case of rain.

**Why it is here.** Once the truck is pouring you can't stop to fetch things. Gone wrong: the vibrator won't start. The tie beam is placed without vibration and comes out of the forms full of honeycombing.

**How to do it.**
1. The day before: test-run the vibrator and fill it with fuel.
2. Count the crew needed for the volume. Ask your concrete sub how many people they bring.
3. Hose and water at the pour. Curing material on site.
4. Tarps and plastic within reach.

**Done looks like.** Everything tested and on site before the first truck arrives.

### 4.8 Weather checked; in heat, pour early

**What it means.** Check the forecast. In hot weather, start the pour early in the morning.

**Why it is here.** Rain on fresh concrete washes the surface and adds water. Heat makes concrete set too fast, tempts the crew to add water, and cracks the surface. Gone wrong: a noon pour in August. The slab sets before it is finished, and fine cracks spread across the surface.

**How to do it.**
1. Check the Bahamas Department of Meteorology forecast the day before and the morning of.
2. Book the first truck for early morning.
3. Rain coming: cover the work, or postpone.
4. In hurricane season, check for storms days ahead. Don't pour if the site needs securing.

**Done looks like.** Forecast checked and noted in the daily report, pour set for the cool of the day.

### 4.9 Cylinders arranged if required

**What it means.** Arrange for concrete test cylinders to be cast on pour day, wherever the engineer requires them.

**Why it is here.** Cylinders prove the strength. If the engineer asks for test results and you have none, the other ways of proving it (drilling cores out of the concrete) cost more and delay the job. Gone wrong: the engineer requires cylinders on the slab. Nobody arranged them. The engineer won't sign off until cores are taken and tested.

**How to do it.**
1. Read the specs, or ask the engineer: "Do you require cylinders on this pour? How many, from which trucks, tested at what ages?"
2. Arrange a testing lab, or ask the supplier if they offer testing. Ask who brings the moulds and who collects them.
3. Label each set with the truck and ticket number.
4. Get the results in writing and file them with the job.

**Done looks like.** Cylinders cast and labelled on the day; lab results filed when they arrive.

### 4.10 Rule told to the crew and driver: no water added without your OK

**What it means.** Before the first truck pours, you tell the driver and the crew out loud: nobody adds water to the concrete without your approval, and then only within what the supplier allows.

**Why it is here.** Water makes concrete easier to place and weaker once it hardens. It is one of the commonest failures in The Bahamas. Gone wrong: the driver adds water "to help you out". The slab tests below strength and the engineer orders cores.

**How to do it.**
1. Say it to the driver and the crew before the pour: "No water goes in this truck without my OK."
2. Check the ticket for water already added at the plant.
3. If the mix is too stiff, call the supplier and ask what is allowed.
4. If someone adds water without permission, write it on the ticket and in the daily report.

**Done looks like.** The rule was given, tickets are noted, and the daily report records any water added and who allowed it.

## Checklist 5. Hurricane: 72 hours out

**Why this checklist exists.** A half-built house is at its weakest. Loose materials become missiles. An open roof fills with rain. Seventy-two hours before landfall is when you act, because by 48 hours you want the site closed and the crew home protecting their own families. The full plan in `05-field-playbook.md`:

| When | Do |
|---|---|
| Before the season | Schedule roof dry-in and openings before August if you can. Confirm insurance covers named storms. Keep the crew contact list current. |
| 5 days out | Stop non-essential deliveries. Check what must be secured. Talk to the client. |
| 72 hours | This checklist. |
| 48 hours | Site closed. Tools and equipment removed or locked. Crew released. |
| After | Safety walk before anyone works. Photograph damage. Notify insurer and client in writing. Send an extension-of-time notice. |

Watch the Bahamas Department of Meteorology and the US National Hurricane Center.

### 5.1 Loose materials and debris secured or removed

**What it means.** Everything that could blow (blocks, plywood, lumber, roofing sheets, buckets, offcuts, trash) is tied down, stored inside, or taken off site.

**Why it is here.** In hurricane winds a sheet of plywood or roofing metal is a missile. Gone wrong: roofing sheets left stacked on the lawn go through the neighbour's windows. Your site, your problem.

**How to do it.**
1. Walk the whole site with a list.
2. Sheets go inside a closed room or the lockable container. Strap lumber stacks. Keep heavy items low; don't stack high.
3. Debris into the skip, then get the skip removed (5.2).
4. Check the neighbours' side of the fence too.

**Done looks like.** A full walk finds nothing loose. Photos taken.

### 5.2 Skip bin emptied or removed

**What it means.** The skip hire company collects or empties the bin.

**Why it is here.** A full skip is a box of missiles, and an empty one can move. Gone wrong: you call at 48 hours and the skip company is booked solid, because every contractor on the island called at the same time.

**How to do it.**
1. Call to book the collection at 5 days out, not at 72 hours.
2. Confirm the collection time in writing.
3. If it can't be removed, empty it, and cover and tie down anything left in it.

**Done looks like.** Skip gone, or empty and secured.

### 5.3 Scaffolding lowered or tied down

**What it means.** Take scaffolding down, or lower it and tie it securely to the structure.

**Why it is here.** Scaffold catches wind like a sail and can fall onto the building, the neighbours or the street. Gone wrong: a scaffold tower falls on the client's car.

**How to do it.**
1. Call your scaffold provider or sub at 5 days out.
2. Remove the planks; they blow away first.
3. Lower to the minimum height and tie into the structure the way the provider instructs, or take it down completely.

**Done looks like.** Scaffold down, or lowered with planks off and tied in. Photo taken.

### 5.4 Open roofs covered and sheathing fastened

**What it means.** Any roof that isn't finished has every sheet of sheathing nailed to the full pattern, and is covered with underlayment or tarps fastened down.

**Why it is here.** Partly nailed sheathing peels off, then the trusses go. An open roof lets rain into the whole house. Gone wrong: sheathing tacked with a few nails, "full nailing next week". The storm peels it off and takes two trusses with it.

**How to do it.**
1. Check every sheet is nailed to the pattern on the drawing, at the edges and in the field (the middle of the sheet).
2. Cover with underlayment fastened per the manufacturer, or tarps held down with wood strips screwed through.
3. Check every truss is braced and strapped.

**Done looks like.** You have checked the roof yourself: fully nailed and covered. Photos taken.

### 5.5 Shutters installed

**What it means.** Hurricane shutters are fitted over all openings. Where the permanent windows aren't in yet, cover the openings with plywood.

**Why it is here.** When a window or door blows in, wind pressure inside the house pushes the roof up from underneath. Gone wrong: a house with its roof on but open window holes. Wind gets in and lifts the roof.

**How to do it.**
1. Before the season, have shutters or pre-cut, labelled plywood panels ready for every opening.
2. Fit them at 72 hours.
3. Use fasteners suited to block and concrete.

**Done looks like.** Every opening covered.

### 5.6 Signs and banners removed

**What it means.** Take down your company sign, banners and anything hanging on the fence or scaffold. Photograph the posted permit, then store it safely.

**Why it is here.** Signs tear free and fly, and you need the permit back on site after the storm.

**How to do it.** Remove them, store them inside, and keep the permit somewhere dry to put back.

**Done looks like.** Nothing hanging on the fence or scaffold. Permit stored dry.

### 5.7 Generator and vehicles fuelled

**What it means.** Fill your vehicles and the generator, and store spare fuel safely.

**Why it is here.** After a storm, fuel stations may be shut or have long lines, and you need to move around and make power for recovery. Gone wrong: the morning after, you can't reach site because the truck is empty and the gas line is three hours long.

**How to do it.**
1. Fill up at 72 hours or earlier.
2. Store generator fuel in proper fuel containers, away from the house and anything that could ignite it.

**Done looks like.** Tanks full, spare fuel stored safely.

### 5.8 Tools and equipment removed or locked

**What it means.** Portable tools go home or to secure storage. Heavy equipment (mixer, compactor) is chained and locked, or locked in the container.

**Why it is here.** Theft rises when sites stand empty, and loose equipment blows or floods. Gone wrong: after the storm the container is open and the tools are gone.

**How to do it.**
1. Keep a tool list. Tick off each item as it leaves or is locked.
2. Store above likely flood level.

**Done looks like.** Every item on the tool list removed or locked.

### 5.9 Whole site photographed

**What it means.** Photos and video of the whole site, from all sides, inside and out, after you have secured it.

**Why it is here.** Insurance claims need proof of the condition before the storm. Gone wrong: the insurer asks whether the damage happened in the storm or was already there. You have no photos.

**How to do it.**
1. Walk every side and every room, plus materials on site.
2. Make sure the photos carry the date.
3. Upload to cloud storage immediately.

**Done looks like.** A dated photo set in cloud storage.

### 5.10 Documents backed up

**What it means.** Contracts, drawings, change orders, daily reports, photos and invoices are all copied to cloud storage.

**Why it is here.** Phones and laptops get wet, lost or go without power for days. Gone wrong: your only copy of the signed change orders is on paper in the truck, and the truck floods.

**How to do it.**
1. Scan or photograph paper documents.
2. Back everything up to cloud storage (Google Drive, OneDrive or similar).
3. Open a file from another device to prove the backup works.

**Done looks like.** Everything in the cloud, tested from a second device.

### 5.11 Client and subs notified; crew released in time

**What it means.** Tell the client what you've done and what happens next. Tell the subs the site is closed. Release your crew by 48 hours so they can protect their own families.

**Why it is here.** People first. And the contract: hurricane delays extend your time (template clause 5.3), but you must give written notice within 7 days. Gone wrong: you keep a crew working at 36 hours to finish a pour. Someone gets hurt, and their family is unprotected.

**How to do it.**
1. Message the client: "Site secured today. Photos attached. Site closed from [date]. We will do a safety walk after the storm passes and report to you."
2. Message every sub: site closed, don't come until you hear from me.
3. Release the crew by 48 hours.
4. After the storm: safety walk before anyone works (power lines, structural damage, standing water). Photograph damage. Notify the insurer and the client in writing within the notice period. Send a written extension-of-time notice. Watch for material price rises and tell the client.
5. Don't raise your prices because people are desperate. Pass on real cost increases with proof. Your reputation lasts longer than the storm.

**Done looks like.** Messages sent and saved, crew home in time, notices sent after the storm.

## Checklist 6. Closeout

**Why this checklist exists.** The last 5% of a job decides whether you collect the last 10% of the money, whether the client refers you, and whether you have proof of the job for licensing.

### 6.1 Punch list walked and signed

**What it means.** Walk the finished work with the client, write down every small item still to fix or finish (the punch list), then sign off each item as it is done.

**Why it is here.** Without a written list, "it's not finished" never ends and the final payment never comes. Gone wrong: the client keeps adding items for weeks and holds back B$4,000.

**How to do it.**
1. Do your own walk first and fix what you find.
2. Give written notice that the work is practically complete (template clause 14.1).
3. Walk with the client within 3 days. Write each item: location, problem, who fixes it, by when.
4. Both sign the list. Anything not on it is not on it.
5. Finish the items within 10 working days (clause 14.2).
6. Walk again and sign off item by item.
7. Remember the template: punch items don't delay the completion payment, except the owner may hold a small agreed amount until they are done.

**Done looks like.** A punch list signed by the client, every item ticked and dated.

### 6.2 Final clean

**What it means.** A thorough clean of everything you worked on: dust, paint spots, stickers, windows, floors, fixtures. Debris and leftover materials gone.

**Why it is here.** The client judges the whole job by how it looks on handover day. Gone wrong: excellent tile work under a film of cement dust. The client says it looks unfinished and calls their friends about the mess, not the tile.

**How to do it.**
1. Book a cleaner or your labourer. Use a room-by-room list.
2. Remove all debris and the skip.
3. Leftover materials: if the client paid for them, ask what they want kept. Otherwise remove them.

**Done looks like.** You would happily photograph it for your portfolio.

### 6.3 Final inspections, certificate of occupancy

**What it means.** Book the final inspections with Building Control and the utilities, and get the certificate of occupancy.

**Why it is here.** Without the certificate of occupancy and utility sign-offs, the client can't get power and water connected, and your final payment usually depends on it. Gone wrong: you book the final at the last minute, an earlier inspection was never signed off, and the client waits three extra weeks to move in.

**How to do it.**
1. Early in the job, ask Building Control what the final inspection covers and which documents they will want.
2. Make sure every earlier inspection was passed and recorded.
3. Book the final. Be there. Fix any notes fast and rebook.
4. Get a copy of the certificate of occupancy for your file.

**Done looks like.** A copy of the certificate of occupancy in the job file.

### 6.4 Warranties, manuals, product list, paint colours handed over

**What it means.** A handover folder for the client, on paper and digital: manufacturer warranties, manuals (AC, appliances, windows, water heater), a list of products and where they were bought, paint brand and colour code for each room, and your workmanship warranty letter.

**Why it is here.** The client needs these to claim warranties and maintain the house. You need them when the client calls in two years about a leak. Gone wrong: the client needs touch-up paint and nobody knows the colour. The AC warranty lapses because nobody registered the unit.

**How to do it.**
1. Collect warranties and manuals as each product is installed, not at the end.
2. Register products where the manufacturer requires it, in the client's name.
3. Make a one-page product and colour list.
4. Hand it over and get the client to sign a receipt for it.

**Done looks like.** The client has signed for the handover folder, and you keep a copy.

### 6.5 Photos of finished work and inside walls (before closing) filed

**What it means.** File the photos of what is inside the walls (taken at rough-in, before the walls were closed) and photos of the finished job.

**Why it is here.** Inside-wall photos are your as-built record of where pipes and wires run. Finished photos are your portfolio and your evidence for licensing. Gone wrong: two years later the client drills into a pipe hanging a mirror and blames your plumber. Your photo shows the pipe exactly where the plan said.

**How to do it.**
1. One folder per job with three subfolders: before, during (inside walls, hold points), after.
2. Name the files with the date and location.
3. Back it up.

**Done looks like.** A complete, backed-up photo folder for the job.

### 6.6 Final invoice; retention date agreed

**What it means.** Send the final invoice. If the client holds retention, agree in writing the date it is released.

**Why it is here.** Money left open with no date drifts forever. Gone wrong: the client held 5% retention "until the end". Eighteen months later it is still unpaid and nobody remembers the agreement.

**How to do it.**
1. Final payment application (`templates/payment-application.md`) listing all change orders.
2. State the retention held and its release date in writing. Usually the end of the 12-month defects period.
3. Put the release date in your calendar.

**Done looks like.** Final invoice sent, retention release date confirmed in writing and in your calendar.

### 6.7 Subs paid; sub retention release dates set

**What it means.** Pay subs for accepted work once the client has paid you for it. Set the dates you will release their 10% retention: half at practical completion, half at the end of the defects period, per your subcontract clause 5.3.

**Why it is here.** Your bench is your business. Late payment to subs travels fast, and the best subs work for the contractors who pay. Gone wrong: you sit on a sub's money for a month after the client paid. Next job, he's "too busy" for you.

**How to do it.**
1. Check each sub's work is accepted and their punch items are done.
2. Pay within the days agreed in the subcontract after you are paid.
3. Write each sub's retention amount and release dates in your tracker.

**Done looks like.** Subs paid, retention schedule recorded.

### 6.8 Testimonial requested

**What it means.** Ask the satisfied client for a short written testimonial and permission to use photos of the work (with no address shown).

**Why it is here.** At 18 with no track record, trust is your scarce resource. Testimonials are proof. Your contract (clause 17.4) needs the client's consent before you use photos for marketing.

**How to do it.**
1. At handover, ask: "Would you write two or three sentences about working with us? And may I use photos of the work, without your address?"
2. Make it easy: send a WhatsApp they can reply to. Ask for a Google review too.

**Done looks like.** The testimonial and photo consent saved in your marketing and licensing file.

### 6.9 Job logged in `my-business/jobs.md` with real cost and lessons

**What it means.** Record the job in `jobs.md`: price, real final cost, gross profit, margin, days planned and actual, date paid in full, photos and testimonial on file, one lesson.

**Why it is here.** This log is your portfolio for licensing, bank loans and future clients, and it feeds your cost book. Lessons not written down get repeated.

**How to do it.**
1. Take the real cost from your job cost tracker (`consultant/tools/job-cost-tracker.xlsx`; see [`05-spreadsheets-explained.md`](05-spreadsheets-explained.md)).
2. Margin = (price - cost) / price.
3. Write one lesson in plain words: what you would do differently.

**Done looks like.** A complete row in `jobs.md`.

## Checklist 7. Monthly business check

**Why this checklist exists.** A contractor can be busy and broke at the same time. One hour a month shows you the truth: cash, tax, who owes you, which jobs made money. Do it the same day every month, before the 15th.

### 7.1 Bank reconciled

**What it means.** Match every line on your business bank statement to your own records (invoices, receipts, accounting software).

**Why it is here.** It catches missed deposits, double payments, bank charges and fraud, and it means your profit numbers are real. Gone wrong: a client's B$2,000 transfer never arrived. You thought it did. Three months later nobody remembers.

**How to do it.**
1. Download the month's statement.
2. Tick each line against your accounting software.
3. Investigate anything that doesn't match.

**Done looks like.** Bank balance equals your books' balance, with every difference explained.

### 7.2 VAT, NIB and business licence reserves in the tax account

**What it means.** Money you owe the government is moved into a separate tax account each time you are paid: VAT you collected (if registered), NIB for payroll, and a reserve for next year's business licence tax.

**Why it is here.** That money is not yours. If it sits in your operating account you will spend it, then face a tax bill with no cash. Gone wrong: you collect B$4,000 of VAT over a quarter, spend it on materials, and can't pay when the return is due.

**How to do it.**
1. Every client payment: move the VAT part straight to the tax account. Example: client pays B$11,000 including B$1,000 VAT. Move B$1,000.
2. Payroll week: move the employer and employee NIB amounts.
3. Business licence reserve: estimate next year's tax. Illustration: if this year's turnover reaches B$180,000 and the reported 0.5% band applied to the whole amount, the tax would be B$900, so reserve B$75 a month. Ask Inland Revenue exactly how the bands are applied.

**Done looks like.** The tax account balance covers everything you owe.

### 7.3 NIB return filed by the 15th (if employer)

**What it means.** If you have employees, the monthly NIB return and payment are due by the 15th of the month.

**Why it is here.** The late penalty is reported at 5% a month, and you need an NIB letter of good standing for government and corporate work. Gone wrong: you forget two months and owe penalties, and a tender closes before you can get your letter.

**How to do it.**
1. Calculate contributions on each employee's wages, up to the ceiling (B$810 a week as of 2026-11-03): employer 6.65% plus employee 4.65% deducted from pay. New rates were expected from 1 July 2026; confirm with NIB.
2. File and pay before the 15th. Ask NIB how returns are filed.
3. Keep proof of payment.

**Done looks like.** Return filed, payment made, receipt saved, before the 15th.

### 7.4 VAT return filed (if registered); rolling 12-month turnover checked against B$100,000

**What it means.** If you are VAT registered, file your VAT return when due. If you are not, add up your taxable sales for the last 12 months every month to see whether you are close to the B$100,000 mandatory registration threshold.

**Why it is here.** Once you pass B$100,000 in any 12 months, registration is mandatory. Missing it means owing VAT you never charged. Gone wrong: you pass the threshold in March without noticing. By August you owe VAT on five months of invoices out of your own pocket.

**How to do it.**
1. Registered: file and pay by the due date Inland Revenue gives you.
2. Not registered: total the last 12 months of sales. Example: 12 months to end of January B$92,000. February invoices B$15,000; last February's B$5,000 drops out. New total = 92,000 - 5,000 + 15,000 = B$102,000. Over: register.
3. If you are getting close, talk to your accountant and Inland Revenue before you cross it.

**Done looks like.** Return filed, or the rolling total written down with how far it is from B$100,000.

### 7.5 Money owed to you listed; late payers chased

**What it means.** A list of every unpaid invoice, who owes it, how much, and how many days late. Chase the late ones.

**Why it is here.** Profit on paper means nothing until it is collected. Gone wrong: three clients each owe B$1,500, 45 days late. That is your next month's overhead sitting in other people's bank accounts.

**How to do it.**
1. List unpaid invoices with their due dates.
2. Day 1 late: polite reminder. Day 7: firm written reminder citing the contract (interest on late payment, right to suspend). Further: formal notice. See `consultant/templates/notice-letters.md`.
3. Track average days to get paid. Under 30 for private clients is healthy.

**Done looks like.** Every late invoice has had action this month, and the list is dated.

### 7.6 Profit per job vs estimate reviewed

**What it means.** For each job, compare actual margin with estimated margin.

**Why it is here.** It shows where your estimates are wrong while there is time to fix the next one. Healthy: within 2 to 3 points of the estimate. Gone wrong: every job estimated at 25% finishes at 15%, and you don't notice for a year.

**How to do it.**
1. From the job cost tracker, compare cost per cost code against the estimate.
2. Note which codes are over and why.
3. Update your cost book with the real numbers.

**Done looks like.** Each job's estimated vs actual margin written down, with the reason for any gap.

### 7.7 Overhead vs budget

**What it means.** Compare what the business actually spent on overhead this month (phone, fuel, insurance, software, marketing, your salary) with what you planned.

**Why it is here.** Overhead creeps up. Every B$100 a month extra needs about B$455 more work at 22% margin just to cover it (100 / 0.22). Gone wrong: subscriptions and fuel creep from B$2,000 to B$2,600 a month. Your break-even jumps from B$9,091 to B$11,818 of monthly revenue without you noticing.

**How to do it.**
1. Total overhead from the accounting software.
2. Compare with the budget line by line.
3. Cut or justify anything over.

**Done looks like.** Overhead total vs budget written down, any overspend explained.

### 7.8 Backlog: how many weeks of signed work?

**What it means.** Add up the signed work not yet done and turn it into weeks or months of work.

**Why it is here.** Healthy backlog is 2 to 3 months. Less means you must sell now. Much more means clients will wait too long, or you are taking on more than you can run. Gone wrong: you are busy for two months, stop quoting, finish everything at once, and have no work for six weeks.

**How to do it.**
1. List signed jobs and the value still to bill on each.
2. Divide by your average monthly revenue. Example: B$45,000 still to bill / B$22,700 a month = about 2 months.

**Done looks like.** Backlog written down in weeks, with a sales plan if it is under 8 weeks.

### 7.9 Sub bench updated

**What it means.** Update `subs.md`: new subs, ratings after each job, document expiry dates, notes.

**Why it is here.** Expired insurance or NIB letters mean a sub can't start, and an un-rated bad sub gets hired again. Gone wrong: a sub's insurance expired in March. He has an accident in May. You never checked.

**How to do it.**
1. Check every document expiry date in the next 60 days and ask for renewals.
2. Rate any sub who finished work this month.
3. Fill gaps: do you have two subs per trade?

**Done looks like.** `subs.md` current, renewals requested, gaps noted.

### 7.10 One thing to improve next month

**What it means.** Pick one specific improvement to the business and do it next month.

**Why it is here.** Small fixes, done every month, compound. Twelve a year is a much better business by year two. Gone wrong: the same mistake (late change orders, forgotten general conditions) repeats on every job because nobody stops to fix the system.

**How to do it.** Look at this month's problems. Pick the one that cost the most. Write the fix as an action with a date: "From 1 December, every change order signed before work. No exceptions."

**Done looks like.** One improvement written down with a date, and last month's improvement checked off.

# Part B. Preconstruction checklist (`05-field-playbook.md`)

**Why this checklist exists.** Preconstruction is everything between the signed contract and the first shovel. Most site problems are really preconstruction problems: no permit, wrong drawings, subs without documents, windows not ordered. Every hour here saves a day on site.

### P1. Contract signed, deposit cleared

**What it means.** Both sides have signed the full contract, and the deposit is cleared funds in your business bank account.

**Why it is here.** No contract means no written scope, payment terms or change rules, and with no lien law the contract is your only security. No cleared deposit means you are spending your own money. Gone wrong: the client signs but "will transfer Monday". You order trusses Friday. Monday the client changes their mind. You own B$6,000 of trusses that fit no other house.

**How to do it.**
1. Use `templates/owner-contract-small-works.md`, filled in completely. Every `[ ]` filled or deleted.
2. Attach Schedule A (scope) and Schedule B (exclusions).
3. Both sign and date. Keep the original and a scan.
4. Check the bank: the deposit shows as cleared, not pending.
5. Only then issue purchase orders.

**Done looks like.** A signed contract in the job file and the deposit visible as cleared in your account.

### P2. Permit issued and posted on site (or confirmed not required, in writing)

**What it means.** The building permit is issued and displayed on site where an inspector can see it. If no permit is needed, you have that in writing from Building Control.

**Why it is here.** Never start before the permit. Work without one can be stopped, fined or ordered removed, and the client may not get a certificate of occupancy. Gone wrong: you start demolition on the promise "the permit is coming this week". It takes another month. An inspector stops the job and your crew moves on to someone else's work.

**How to do it.**
1. Get a copy of the issued permit and the approved, stamped plans that go with it.
2. Ask Building Control how the permit must be displayed, and post it that way, protected from rain.
3. If no permit is needed, keep the email or letter that says so in the job file.
4. Note the permit number in the daily report and the job file.

**Done looks like.** Permit posted on site, copy in the file, or a written "not required" on file.

### P3. Current drawings, stamped where required; you have read every sheet

**What it means.** You have the latest version of every drawing, stamped by the architect or engineer where the work is structural, and you have personally read every sheet.

**Why it is here.** Building from an old revision is one of the easiest expensive mistakes. Structural sizes come only from stamped drawings. And conflicts between sheets are found by reading, not by pouring. Gone wrong: the block sub builds from revision A. Revision B moved a window 2 feet. Cut out and rebuild.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask the architect or client: "Which is the latest revision of each sheet?" Get a list with dates.
2. Mark old copies "SUPERSEDED" and remove them from site.
3. Read every sheet: site plan, floor plans, elevations, sections, structural, electrical, plumbing, schedules, notes.
4. Write down every question or conflict and send them as numbered RFIs (requests for information).
5. Give each sub only the current sheets for their work.

**Done looks like.** A drawing list with revision dates, only current sets on site, your RFIs sent and answered.

### P4. Survey or boundary pegs located; setbacks confirmed (get a surveyor for new work)

**What it means.** You know where the property boundaries are, and you have checked the building will sit inside the Town Planning setbacks.

**Why it is here.** Setbacks are measured from the true boundary, not from an old fence. Gone wrong: you set out an addition from the neighbour's fence line. The survey later shows the fence is 3 feet onto your client's land the other way, and the addition is in the setback. Refusal, a neighbour dispute, maybe demolition.

**How to do it.**
1. For new work, the client hires a licensed land surveyor to locate or reset the boundary pegs (or you arrange one as part of your scope).
2. Find every peg on site and protect them. Flag them so nobody knocks them out.
3. Measure from the pegs to where the building will go, using the setbacks on the approved site plan.
4. If anything doesn't fit, stop and send an RFI before you dig.

**Done looks like.** Pegs found and flagged, setback measurements written down and matching the approved plan.

### P5. Existing services located: water, power, septic, cable

**What it means.** You know where every existing pipe, cable, septic tank and soak-away is before anyone digs or cuts.

**Why it is here.** Hitting a live cable can kill someone. Hitting a water main floods the site. Driving a machine over a septic tank can collapse it. Gone wrong: the backhoe tears out the water line to the client's house. They have no water for two days, and you pay for the repair.

**How to do it.**
1. Ask the client: "Where do the water line, power cable, septic tank and soak-away run? Any old ones?"
2. Look for clues: meters, cleanouts, manholes, poles, cable boxes, wet patches.
3. Ask BPL, Water & Sewerage and the cable company how to get their lines located or marked before you dig.
4. Mark what you find on the ground (paint or pegs) and on your site plan.
5. Dig by hand near anything marked.

**Done looks like.** Services marked on the ground and on your site plan before any machine digs.

### P6. Site plan: deliveries, storage, skip bin, toilet, parking, neighbours, security

**What it means.** A simple sketch of the site showing where deliveries arrive, where materials are stored, where the skip bin and temporary toilet go, where people park, how you protect neighbours, and how the site is secured.

**Why it is here.** Without a plan, materials get dumped in the way, the skip blocks the drive, and the block delivery lands on the septic tank. Gone wrong: the block truck tips 2,000 blocks across the only access. Everything must be moved by hand before the concrete truck can get in.

**How to do it.**
1. Sketch the lot (from the survey or by hand) with the building outline.
2. Mark: delivery point, storage area (flat, firm, not over services), skip bin, toilet, parking, lockable container, gate.
3. Note neighbours: who to tell about noise, deliveries and pours.
4. Plan security: fencing, a lockable container, just-in-time deliveries, nothing valuable left out overnight.
5. Share it with subs and suppliers.

**Done looks like.** A one-page site plan sketch in the job file and with every sub.

### P7. Subcontracts signed, documents collected (licence, NIB, insurance, crew list)

**What it means.** Every sub has a signed subcontract and has given you their business licence, NIB letter of good standing, insurance certificate naming you, crew list, and trade licence where needed.

**Why it is here.** This is checklist 2 put into action. A sub without a subcontract has no rules. A sub without documents is a risk to you. Gone wrong: the plumber starts before you collect his insurance certificate. He floods the client's living room. He has no insurance.

**How to do it.**
1. Use the `subs.md` columns as your tick list. Every document column must say "yes".
2. Hold a pre-start meeting with each sub: scope, drawings, schedule, crew size, site rules, quality standard, how they get paid.
3. No documents, no start.

**Done looks like.** Every sub on the job shows "yes" in every document column, and a signed subcontract is on file.

### P8. Long-lead items ordered; lead times on the schedule

**What it means.** Items that take weeks to arrive are ordered right after the deposit clears, and their delivery dates are on your schedule.

**Why it is here.** Typical lead times to Nassau: impact windows and doors 8 to 16 weeks, trusses 2 to 6 weeks, AC and generators 2 to 8 weeks (longer before hurricane season), custom cabinets 4 to 10 weeks. Family Islands add 1 to 3 weeks. Gone wrong: windows are ordered when the walls go up instead of at signing. The house stands with open holes for two months, into hurricane season.

**How to do it.**
1. List every long-lead item from the drawings and specs.
2. Confirm sizes in writing. Windows and doors need measured rough openings or confirmed drawing sizes, and a signed submittal from the client.
3. Order the week the contract is signed and the deposit clears.
4. Get a written delivery date from the supplier. Put it on the schedule.
5. Call the supplier every two weeks to confirm the date still holds.

**Done looks like.** Purchase orders issued, written delivery dates, and those dates on the schedule.

### P9. Schedule built and sent to client and subs

**What it means.** A schedule (a bar chart is fine) from start to handover, showing each task, who does it, and when, plus inspections and deliveries. Sent to the client and every sub.

**Why it is here.** Without a schedule, subs show up when it suits them, inspections get booked late, and the client has no idea when to make selections. Gone wrong: the plumber and the tile setter both arrive on the same Monday for the same bathroom. One leaves, and doesn't come back for two weeks.

**How to do it.**
1. Start from the 26-step sequence in [`03-how-a-house-gets-built.md`](03-how-a-house-gets-built.md), or the task list in your estimate.
2. Give each task a duration (ask the sub), a start and an end.
3. Add inspections, long-lead deliveries, client selection deadlines, and hurricane-season risk.
4. Send it. Then update the three-week look-ahead every week.

**Done looks like.** A dated schedule sent to the client and all subs, and the first three-week look-ahead out.

### P10. Budget set from the estimate; cost codes ready

**What it means.** Turn your estimate into a job budget, broken down by cost code (01 General conditions, 03 Concrete, 04 Masonry and so on; full list in `consultant/knowledge/07-cost-codes.md`), so every bill can be coded and tracked.

**Why it is here.** If you don't set the budget per code at the start, you can't tell where money leaks until the job is over. Gone wrong: the job finishes B$3,000 over. You have no idea whether it was the concrete, the tile or the skip bins, so you make the same mistake next time.

**How to do it.**
1. Copy the estimate lines into the job cost tracker (`consultant/tools/job-cost-tracker.xlsx`; see [`05-spreadsheets-explained.md`](05-spreadsheets-explained.md)) under their cost codes.
2. Record buyout: enter signed subcontract and purchase order amounts as committed cost.
3. Code every invoice and receipt as it arrives.

**Done looks like.** A budget per cost code in the tracker, with committed costs entered after buyout.

### P11. Kickoff meeting with client: who decides, how changes work, selection deadlines, payment dates, communication (weekly update with photos)

**What it means.** A meeting with the client before work starts, to agree how the job will run.

**Why it is here.** Most client fights come from different expectations, not bad work. Settle them on day zero. Gone wrong: the client thinks "small changes" are free, chooses tile two weeks late, and expects daily calls. You expected none of that. Both sides are angry by week three.

**How to do it.** Cover these and confirm them in writing after:
1. **Who decides:** one point of contact on each side. Who can sign change orders.
2. **Changes:** no change without a signed change order with price and time. Explain why.
3. **Selections:** list every selection (tile, fixtures, paint, cabinets) with its deadline from the schedule. Late selections extend time.
4. **Payment dates:** walk through each milestone and the amount.
5. **Communication:** a weekly update with photos, decisions needed with dates, and money status. Bad news early, with a fix.
6. **Site rules:** working hours, access, toilet, where the client should and shouldn't go.

**Done looks like.** A short written summary sent to the client the same day, and their "OK" in reply.

### P12. Hurricane plan if any work falls June 1 to November 30

**What it means.** A written hurricane plan for the job if any work falls inside hurricane season.

**Why it is here.** A storm can come with five days' warning or fewer. If you have no plan, you are making it up while everyone else is buying plywood. Gone wrong: a storm forms in October. You have no shutters ready, no crew contact list, and the insurer says the policy has no named-storm cover.

**How to do it.**
1. Copy the hurricane plan from `05-field-playbook.md` into the job file.
2. Add job specifics: what is most at risk (open roof, scaffold, materials), where materials can be stored inside, who does what.
3. Confirm the insurance covers named storms. Get it in writing from the broker.
4. Schedule roof dry-in and openings before August if you can.
5. Keep crew, sub, client and supplier contacts current in one list.
6. Use checklist 5 when a storm is forecast.

**Done looks like.** A one-page job hurricane plan on file, insurance confirmed in writing, and the contact list current.

# Part D. Quality hold points (`05-field-playbook.md`)

**What a hold point is.** A point where work stops until a check is done and passed: first by you, then by Building Control where the permit requires it. Once concrete, block, drywall or tile covers something, it is buried. Checking takes minutes; fixing takes thousands.

**How every hold point works.**
1. Do your own check with the stamped drawing in your hand.
2. Fix everything. Photograph it with a tape measure in the shot.
3. Call Building Control where required. Walk with the inspector.
4. Record it in the daily report: your check, the inspector's result, the time and the inspector's name.
5. Only then let the next trade cover it.

Structural sizes, bar sizes, spacings, laps, cover and connections come from the engineer's stamped drawings. The checks below tell you what to look at. The drawings tell you what the right answer is.

### H1. Footings

**What the check means.**
- **Depth and width per drawing:** the trench is as deep and wide as the drawing says, all along.
- **Bottom firm, clean, no loose fill or water:** the base is solid ground or rock, not loose soil, debris or standing water.
- **Rebar size, spacing, laps:** see 4.2.
- **3 inch cover on chairs or blocks, not on the dirt:** bars held 3 inches clear of the ground and trench sides.
- **Dowels for walls in the right place:** the bars sticking up out of the footing line up exactly with where the block cores will be.

**Why it is here.** The footing carries the whole house. A footing on loose fill settles and cracks the walls. Dowels in the wrong place mean the wall bars can't connect to the footing, which breaks the load path. Gone wrong: dowels set by eye, 4 inches off the wall line. They fall in the middle of a block web instead of a core. The crew bends them over to fit. The wall is no longer tied to its foundation the way it was designed.

**How to do it.**
1. Measure depth from your level line at several points along every trench. Measure width.
2. Look at the bottom. Push a bar in. It should be firm. No mud, no water, no loose soil or debris. Clean it out if needed.
3. Check rebar as in 4.2.
4. Measure cover: 3 inches from bar to soil on every side.
5. Pull a string line along the wall line. Check each dowel sits on it, at the spacing the drawing shows, so it will fall in a block core.
6. Then call Building Control.

**Done looks like.** Your check and Building Control's pass recorded, photos of trench depth, rebar, cover and dowels, before any concrete.

### H2. Block walls

**What the check means.**
- **Plumb, level, line:** walls vertical, courses horizontal, faces straight.
- **Mortar joints full:** no gaps in the joints between blocks.
- **Vertical bars at the right spacing and lapped:** bars in the cores at the positions and spacing on the drawing (corners, openings, and along the wall), with laps to the dowels as specified.
- **Cleanouts at the bottom of grouted cores:** openings at the base of each core to be filled, to clear mortar droppings and check the bar.
- **Lintels and columns per drawing:** beams over openings and any concrete columns are the size and type drawn.

**Why it is here.** A wall out of plumb is weaker and looks bad forever under stucco. Empty joints leak and weaken the wall. Missing bars or short laps mean the wall can't resist hurricane loads. Gone wrong: the crew skips the bars at a window jamb "because it's a short wall". That is exactly where the wind load concentrates.

**How to do it.**
1. Hold a 4 ft level against the wall face and top at several places. Sight down the wall for straightness. Ask what tolerance the drawings or specs allow.
2. Look at the joints from both sides. Full mortar, no daylight through gaps.
3. Count and measure bar positions against the drawing. Check laps.
4. Look through each cleanout before filling: bar in place, no mortar heap at the bottom.
5. Check lintel sizes, bearing (how far they sit on the wall at each end) and type against the drawing.

**Done looks like.** Checks recorded, photos of bars and cleanouts, defects fixed before core filling.

### H3. Core fill

**What the check means.**
- **Cores filled solid, no voids:** every core that should be filled is full from bottom to top.
- **Rodded or vibrated:** air worked out with a rod or vibrator.
- **Filled in lifts, not dumped from the top of a tall wall:** placed in stages of height and consolidated each time.

**Why it is here.** Hollow or half-filled cores are one of the commonest failures in The Bahamas. A filled core with its bar is a column. A hollow one is just block. Gone wrong: concrete dumped from the top of a 9 ft wall hangs up on mortar fins halfway down. The bottom 3 feet stays empty. Nobody knows until the wall cracks in a storm.

**How to do it.**
1. Check the cleanouts were cleared and closed.
2. Watch the fill. Concrete placed in lifts, each one rodded or vibrated before the next.
3. Tap the block face with a hammer at the filled cores. A dull sound means full. A hollow ring can mean a void. Investigate.
4. Check the top of each filled core is full to the level the drawing shows.

**Done looks like.** You watched the fill, tapped the cores, and recorded it with photos.

### H4. Tie beam

**What the check means.**
- **Bar size and count, stirrups:** the right number and size of main bars, with stirrups (loops around them) at the spacing drawn.
- **Laps at corners:** bars continue around corners and lap as detailed, so the beam is one continuous ring.
- **Cover:** bars held clear of the forms by the cover shown, using chairs or spacers.
- **Straps or anchors set at every truss position before the pour:** the hurricane straps are in.
- **Formwork braced; top level:** forms won't move, and the top of the beam is level for the roof to sit on.

**Why it is here.** The tie beam ties the walls together and holds the roof down. Straps forgotten before the pour, then retrofitted badly, is a classic Bahamian failure, and weak roof-to-wall connections are the main cause of roof loss in hurricanes. Gone wrong: the truss layout changed after the straps were set. Six straps are in the wrong places. The roof is fixed with improvised connections nobody designed.

**How to do it.**
1. Count main bars and check sizes. Measure stirrup spacing.
2. At every corner, check the corner bars and laps against the detail.
3. Check cover all round with a tape.
4. Lay out the latest truss drawing. Mark each truss position. Check a strap at each, the right type, set to the right depth, tied in. Count straps against trusses.
5. Push on the forms. Check the top with a level or laser.
6. Then call Building Control.

**Done looks like.** Strap count equals truss count, your check and Building Control's pass recorded, photos of every corner and a sample of straps.

### H5. Concrete (every pour)

**What the check means.**
- **Ordered strength matches the drawing:** see 4.5.
- **Check delivery ticket:** every truck's ticket shows the right mix and strength, and any water added at the plant.
- **No water added on site beyond what the supplier allows:** see 4.10.
- **Vibrate:** every part of the pour is vibrated to remove air.
- **Finish:** the surface is finished as specified (screed, float, trowel for slabs).
- **Keep wet and cure 7 days:** see the glossary entry **cure**.
- **Cylinders taken where the engineer requires:** see 4.9.

**Why it is here.** Every part of this list prevents a weak pour: wrong mix, too much water, air pockets, a surface that dries too fast in the sun. Gone wrong: the slab is poured perfectly, then left in the August sun with no curing. By the next morning it is crazed with cracks and the surface dusts off under a broom.

**How to do it.**
1. Run checklist 4 before every pour.
2. Read every ticket before that truck pours. Keep them.
3. Watch the vibrator work through the whole pour, especially corners and around bars.
4. Start curing as soon as the surface can take it: wet burlap, sprinkler or plastic sheeting. Keep it wet for 7 days. Assign a person to do it, and check.

**Done looks like.** Tickets filed, curing done for 7 days and recorded in the daily reports, cylinder results filed if required.

### H6. Roof framing

**What the check means.**
- **Truss spacing:** trusses at the spacing shown on the truss drawings.
- **Bracing:** temporary and permanent bracing installed as the truss drawings show.
- **Every strap nailed with the right nails and count:** each strap wrapped and nailed to the truss with the nail type and number the drawing or strap maker specifies.
- **Sheathing nailing pattern at edges and field per drawing:** the roof deck nailed at the spacing drawn, closer at edges than in the middle.

**Why it is here.** This is the roof-to-wall connection, the main cause of roof loss in hurricanes. A strap with three nails where eight are specified holds a fraction of the load. Gone wrong: the framer uses short, smooth nails because they were on the truck. Every strap is under-nailed. The inspector fails it, or worse, nobody notices.

**How to do it.**
1. Measure truss spacing at several places.
2. Compare the bracing with the truss drawing.
3. Check every strap. Count the nails. Check the nail type (length, ring-shank where specified, galvanised).
4. Walk the sheathing before it is covered. Check nail spacing at edges and field with a tape. Look for nails that missed the truss (shiners poking through underneath).
5. Then call Building Control for the roof framing and strapping inspection.

**Done looks like.** Every strap checked and photographed, sheathing nailing checked, Building Control's pass recorded.

### H7. Roofing

**What the check means.**
- **Underlayment laps:** each row of underlayment overlaps the one below by the amount the manufacturer specifies, so water runs over the joint, not into it.
- **Drip edge:** metal edging installed along the eaves and rakes.
- **High-wind nailing or fastening pattern from the manufacturer:** shingles or metal fixed with the number and position of fasteners the manufacturer requires for high-wind areas.

**Why it is here.** A roof installed to the normal pattern instead of the high-wind pattern loses shingles in the first strong storm, and the manufacturer's warranty may not cover it. Gone wrong: four nails per shingle instead of the six the high-wind instructions call for. Half the roof lifts in a tropical storm.

**How to do it.**
1. Get the manufacturer's installation instructions for high-wind areas before the roofer starts. Give a copy to the roofer.
2. Check underlayment laps with a tape before shingles go on.
3. Check drip edge at all edges.
4. Lift a few shingles (or check fastener rows on metal) in several places and count fasteners against the instructions.

**Done looks like.** Instructions on file, laps and fastening checked and photographed, manufacturer warranty registered.

### H8. Windows and doors

**What the check means.**
- **Product approved for the opening:** the window or door has a product approval showing it meets the wind and impact rating needed for that opening.
- **Anchors type and spacing per product approval:** fixed into the block or concrete with the anchors and spacing the approval document specifies.
- **Flashing and sealant:** water is directed out at the head and sill, and the frame is sealed to the wall.
- **Operate and lock:** every window and door opens, closes and locks properly.

**Why it is here.** An impact window is only rated if it is installed exactly as tested. No flashing at windows is one of the commonest leaks in Bahamian block houses. Gone wrong: the installer uses half the anchors because the block is "solid enough". The window passes a rain test but could blow in at hurricane loads. The rating is gone.

**How to do it.**
1. Get the product approval document for each window and door before installation. Check it covers the size installed.
2. Give the installation and anchoring details to the installer.
3. Check anchor type, embedment and spacing on several units. Count them.
4. Check flashing at the head and sill before the stucco covers it.
5. Open, close and lock every unit.

**Done looks like.** Approvals on file, anchoring and flashing photographed before stucco, every unit operated.

### H9. Wet areas

**What the check means.**
- **Waterproof membrane:** the shower walls and floor (and other wet areas as specified) are covered with a waterproof membrane, installed per the manufacturer.
- **Flood tested 24 hours before tile:** the waterproofed area is filled with water and left 24 hours to prove it doesn't leak.
- **Falls to drain:** the floor slopes toward the drain so water doesn't pond.

**Why it is here.** A shower that leaks behind the tile rots the wall, grows mould and soaks the room next door. You only find out months later, and the fix is ripping out the tile. Gone wrong: no flood test. Six months after handover, the bedroom wall next to the shower shows a damp stain. You remove and replace all the tile at your cost.

**How to do it.**
1. Check the membrane covers everything specified, with laps and corners done per the manufacturer.
2. Plug the drain. Fill the shower floor with water to the height the manufacturer or spec gives. Mark the water level.
3. Wait 24 hours. Check the level hasn't dropped, and check below and beside for damp.
4. Before tile, check the falls: pour a little water and watch it run to the drain, or use a level.

**Done looks like.** Flood test recorded in the daily report with photos at start and end, falls checked, before any tile goes on.

### H10. MEP

**What the check means.**
- **Licensed trades only:** electrical and plumbing work done by licensed electricians and plumbers.
- **Inspected before covering:** rough-in inspected by Building Control before walls, ceilings or slabs close over it.
- **Photos of everything inside walls before closing:** every wall and ceiling photographed with the pipes, wires and boxes visible.

**Why it is here.** Once drywall or plaster covers the rough-in, problems are hidden and inspections can't be done. Gone wrong: the drywall crew closes a wall before the electrical inspection. The inspector makes you open it. You pay twice for that wall.

**How to do it.**
1. Confirm the licence of every electrician and plumber (2.4).
2. Book the rough-in inspections. Tell the drywall and plaster subs, in writing, not to close any wall until you say so.
3. Photograph every wall and ceiling, with a tape measure in the shot for position.
4. After the pass, tell the closing trades they can start.

**Done looks like.** Rough-in inspections passed and recorded, a full set of inside-wall photos filed, and walls closed only after both.
