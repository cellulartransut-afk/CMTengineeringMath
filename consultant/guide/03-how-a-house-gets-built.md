# 03. How a Bahamian concrete block house gets built

Rev 1 · 2026-11-04

## What this guide is

A walk through the 26-step house sequence in [`consultant/knowledge/05-field-playbook.md`](../knowledge/05-field-playbook.md), written for someone who has never stood on a building site. For each step: what happens, which materials and trades are involved, what the parts are called, what the inspector looks at, how long it typically takes, and what you check as the super.

Read it with [`01-glossary.md`](01-glossary.md) open. The item-by-item checks are in [`02-checklists-explained.md`](02-checklists-explained.md), Part D.

**One rule before anything else.** Structural sizes come from the stamped drawings: footing size, bar sizes and spacing, laps, cover, beam sizes, strap types, nailing, concrete strength. Not from you, not from the sub, not from "how we always do it", not from this guide. This guide tells you what to look at. The engineer's drawings tell you what the right answer is. If the drawings are unclear, send an RFI (a written question) and wait for a written answer.

**About the times.** Durations below are typical for a simple single-storey house of about 1,200 to 1,500 sq ft in New Providence, with steady subs and normal weather. They vary a lot. Your real durations come from your subs, your crew sizes, the weather and the inspectors. Family Islands are slower.

## Part 1. The house in one picture

Read from the ground up. This is a section, as if you sliced through an outside wall.

```
              roof covering (shingles or metal)
              underlayment (waterproof layer)
              sheathing (plywood or OSB roof deck)
      /\      trusses or rafters  <-- each one strapped down
     /  \
 =====================================  TIE BEAM (reinforced concrete,
 |  hurricane straps cast in here    |  runs all the way round)
 |                                   |
 |  BLOCK WALL (8 inch block)        |  <-- vertical bars in cores
 |  lintel over each opening         |      filled solid with concrete
 |  window, with flashing            |
 |                                   |
 ===== SLAB (concrete floor) ========|  on vapour barrier, on treated,
 |  STEM WALL (block)                |  compacted fill
 =====================================
 |      FOOTING (reinforced concrete strip)     |
 ------------------------------------------------
                firm ground
```

### The load path: why every part is connected

Wind in a hurricane does three things to a house: pushes on the walls, sucks up on the roof, and tries to slide the whole building. The house survives only if every part is connected to the part below it, all the way to the ground. That chain is the **load path**:

1. Roof covering is fastened to the sheathing.
2. Sheathing is nailed to the trusses.
3. Each truss is strapped to the tie beam.
4. The tie beam is tied to the vertical bars in the walls.
5. The vertical bars sit in filled cores and lap onto dowels in the footing.
6. The footing sits on firm ground.

Break one link (a missing strap, a hollow core, a short lap) and that is where the house fails. Almost every check in this guide protects a link in that chain.

## Part 2. Why Bahamian houses are built this way

| Threat | What it does | How the house answers it |
|---|---|---|
| **Hurricanes** | Wind lifts roofs, pushes walls, turns debris into missiles. The code's design wind speed is widely cited as 150 mph. | Reinforced block walls with filled cores, a continuous tie beam, a hurricane strap at every truss, impact-rated windows and doors or shutters (the 3rd edition code made shutters mandatory), roofing fixed to the manufacturer's high-wind pattern. |
| **Termites** | Eat wood, quietly, for years. | Concrete and block walls (termites can't eat them). Soil treated before the slab. No wood touching soil. Pressure-treated lumber where specified. |
| **Salt air** | Rusts steel. Rusting rebar swells and breaks the concrete off (spalling). | Enough concrete cover over all rebar. Washed sand only, never beach sand. Hot-dip galvanised or stainless straps, connectors and fasteners near the coast. Stucco and paint as a weather skin. |
| **Heat and sun** | Dries concrete too fast (weak, cracked). Breaks down sealants and paint. Puts workers at risk. | Pour early in the day. Keep concrete wet for 7 days. UV-rated sealants and exterior paint. Water and shade on site. |
| **Humidity and mould** | Damp walls, mould behind showers and in closets. | Vapour barrier under the slab. Waterproof membranes in showers. Moisture-resistant board in wet areas. AC sized and drained properly. |
| **Imports** | Almost every material comes from Florida: freight, duty, lead times. | Block and ready-mix concrete are made on the main islands, so block is the practical wall. Lumber, windows, roofing and finishes are imported, so order early and price them landed. |

The short version: a block house with a tie beam and strapped roof survives storms, termites can't eat it, and the materials are available. That is why nearly every house you will work on is built this way.

## Part 4. What an inspection is

There are two kinds, and you need both.

**Your inspection (the hold point).** Before you call anyone, you check the work yourself against the stamped drawings. You fix what is wrong. You photograph it. You write it in the daily report. This is the super's job, and nobody else will do it for you.

**The Building Control inspection.** An inspector from the Ministry of Public Works comes to site at set stages and checks that the work matches the approved drawings and the Building Code. Typical stages: footings before pour, slab before pour, tie beam before pour, roof framing and straps, electrical rough, plumbing rough, and final. Your permit sets the stages for your job.

How it works, in practice:
1. You finish your own check and fix everything.
2. You book the inspection. Ask Building Control at the start of the job how inspections are booked, how much notice they need, and what record you receive when work passes.
3. The inspector visits. Be there. Walk with them with the drawings in hand.
4. They pass it, or give you corrections. Fix the corrections and rebook.
5. Nothing gets covered or poured until it has passed.

If an inspection is delayed, follow up politely in writing and record it in the daily report; inspection delays not caused by you extend your time under the contract. Never offer anything to "speed it up". That is a bribe, it is illegal, and in a country this small it will follow you for life.

## Part 5. The 26 steps

### Step 1. Permit, set out (pegs, string lines, levels)

**What happens.** First, the permit. Town Planning approves the use and position; Building Control reviews the plans and issues the building permit. Nothing starts before it. Then **setting out**: marking on the ground exactly where the building goes. The surveyor's boundary pegs are found. From them, using the setbacks on the approved site plan, the corners of the house are marked with pegs. Batter boards (small timber frames) go up a few feet outside each corner, and string lines are stretched between them to mark the wall lines. A **benchmark** (a fixed point of known height) is set, and the floor level is marked from it.

**Materials and trades.** Surveyor; you or the block foreman. Pegs, timber for batter boards, string line, nails, tape measures, a level (laser or builder's level), marking paint.

**Words you'll hear.** Set out, pegs, batter boards, string lines, benchmark, finished floor level, square, diagonals, setbacks.

**Inspection.** No Building Control inspection is usual at this stage (check your permit). Your own check is critical, because everything after this is built from these lines.

**Typical time (varies).** Permit: an older benchmark put Building Control at about 120 days; complete drawings and same-day answers to queries shorten it. Set out: 1 to 2 days.

**The super checks.**
- The permit is issued and posted (P2 in the checklists guide).
- Distances from boundary pegs to the building corners match the setbacks on the approved plan.
- The building is square. Measure both diagonals; they must be equal. A 30 x 40 ft rectangle has diagonals of exactly 50 ft (the 3-4-5 triangle: 3 x 10, 4 x 10, 5 x 10).
- The benchmark is somewhere it won't be knocked, and you have written down its height.
- Existing services (water, power, septic, cable) are located and marked before anyone digs (P5).

### Step 2. Excavate footings

**What happens.** Trenches are dug along the string lines for the footings: the concrete strips that carry the walls. Topsoil and anything soft or organic is removed until the bottom is firm ground. In much of The Bahamas you hit limestone rock close to the surface, which may need breaking or cutting.

**Materials and trades.** Excavator or backhoe operator (or labourers by hand for small work), labourers to trim the trenches. Truck to haul spoil (the dug-out material) if it can't stay on site.

**Words you'll hear.** Trench, spoil, firm bottom, rock, over-dig, dewatering (pumping out water).

**Inspection.** The footing inspection comes at step 3, after the steel is in.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 3 days. Longer in hard rock.

**The super checks.**
- Trench depth and width match the drawing all along, measured from your benchmark, not from the uneven ground.
- The bottom is firm, with no loose soil, mud, debris or standing water.
- If you hit something unexpected (soft pockets, buried debris, an old septic, a lot of rock), stop, photograph, and tell the client and engineer. Rock excavation and buried debris are normally exclusions, so they become a change order.
- Trench sides are stable. Over 5 feet deep, shore or slope the sides. Nobody goes into an unsupported trench.

### Step 3. Hold: footing inspection (yours, then Building Control)

**What happens.** Rebar for the footing is placed in the trench on chairs or concrete blocks, with **dowels** (short bars) standing up where the wall's vertical bars will go. Then everything stops for inspection.

**Materials and trades.** Steel fixer or block crew. Rebar, tie wire, chairs or dobies.

**Words you'll hear.** Hold point, cage, chairs, dobies, cover, laps, dowels, tie wire.

**Inspection.** Building Control checks the footing against the approved drawings: trench size and bottom, rebar size and spacing, laps, cover, and dowel positions.

**Typical time (varies).** Your check: an hour or two. Building Control: depends on their notice period; ask when you start the job and book ahead.

**The super checks.** Hold point H1 in full:
- Depth and width per drawing.
- Bottom firm, clean, no loose fill or water.
- Rebar size, spacing and laps per drawing.
- 3 inch cover, bars on chairs or blocks, not on the dirt.
- Dowels on the wall line, at the spacing drawn, so each one will land in a block core. Pull a string line to check.
- Photos of everything, with a tape measure in the shot.

### Step 4. Pour footings

**What happens.** Concrete is placed in the trench, vibrated, and struck level at the top. The dowels must stay exactly where they were set.

**Materials and trades.** Ready-mix supplier (or site-mixed concrete for very small jobs), concrete crew, possibly a pump. Vibrator, shovels, screed boards.

**Words you'll hear.** Ready-mix, delivery ticket, slump, strength, vibrate, cold joint, cure.

**Inspection.** None during the pour, but your concrete checklist applies to every pour.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 day. Then cure.

**The super checks.** Run checklist 4 (before any concrete pour) and hold point H5:
- The inspection passed before the truck was ordered to pour.
- The ticket on every truck matches the strength on the drawing.
- No water added on site without your OK.
- Concrete vibrated, top level, dowels still in place and upright.
- Kept wet afterwards. Cure 7 days.

### Step 5. Block up to slab level (stem walls); fill cores at rebar

**What happens.** The block crew lays block on the footing, up to the height of the future floor slab. These short walls are **stem walls**. Vertical bars are lapped onto the dowels, and the cores (hollow centres of the blocks) where the bars sit are filled solid with concrete.

**Materials and trades.** Block crew. 8 inch block (or as drawn), mortar (cement, washed sand, water), rebar, concrete or grout for core fill.

**Words you'll hear.** Course (one row of blocks), mortar joint, plumb, level, line, cores, core fill, cleanout, lap.

**Inspection.** Not usually a separate Building Control stage (check your permit). Your own hold point checks apply.

**Typical time (varies).** 3 to 6 days, plus a day for core fill.

**The super checks.** Hold points H2 and H3:
- First course laid on the string line, level.
- Walls plumb and level; joints full.
- Vertical bars at every dowel, lapped to the length on the drawing.
- Cleanouts at the bottom of cores to be filled, cleared of mortar, then closed.
- Cores filled solid, in lifts, rodded or vibrated. Tap the face; a hollow ring means a void.

### Step 6. Backfill and compact in layers; termite treatment

**What happens.** Fill material is placed inside the stem walls, and against the outside of the foundation, to bring the ground up to just under slab level. It goes in layers, each one compacted with a plate compactor or roller before the next. When the fill is at level, a pest control company treats the soil for termites.

**Materials and trades.** Fill material (as specified), compactor, labourers, water for compaction where the spec calls for it. Pest control company.

**Words you'll hear.** Backfill, fill, compaction, plate compactor ("whacker"), lifts or layers, soil treatment, termiticide.

**Inspection.** Not usually a Building Control stage on its own (check your permit). The engineer may specify compaction requirements or testing.

**Typical time (varies).** 2 to 5 days of fill and compaction. Termite treatment: usually part of a day.

**The super checks.**
- Fill is the material specified, clean, with no wood, roots or rubbish. Wood in fill feeds termites.
- Layers are thin enough to compact (thickness per the drawings or engineer) and each is compacted. You can't compact a deep pile from the top.
- The fill is at the right level: slab thickness below finished floor, checked from your benchmark.
- Termite treatment applied over the whole area, by a pest control company, after the final compaction and before the vapour barrier. Get a written record of what was applied and when, for your file and the client's handover folder.

### Step 7. Under-slab plumbing and electrical sleeves; vapour barrier; slab rebar or mesh on chairs

**What happens.** Everything that must go under or through the slab goes in now. The plumber lays the drain pipes and any water lines that run under the floor, and the plumber or electrician sets **sleeves** and **conduits** where pipes and wires will pass through the slab. Then the **vapour barrier** (heavy plastic sheet) is laid over the treated fill, with laps overlapped and taped. Then the slab reinforcement (rebar or welded wire mesh) goes on chairs above the barrier, and the slab edge forms are set.

**Materials and trades.** Licensed plumber, electrician, steel fixer, carpenter for edge forms. PVC drain pipe and fittings, sleeves, conduit, vapour barrier, tape, rebar or mesh, chairs, form lumber.

**Words you'll hear.** Under-slab, drain lines, stub-ups (pipe ends sticking up where fixtures will go), sleeves, conduit, vapour barrier, mesh, chairs, edge forms, thickened edge.

**Inspection.** The plumber's under-slab work is often tested and inspected before it is buried. Ask your plumber and Building Control what test and inspection they want at this stage. The slab inspection itself is step 8.

**Typical time (varies).** 3 to 6 days.

**The super checks.**
- Every stub-up is in the right position for its fixture (toilet, shower, sink) per the plumbing drawing. Measure from the walls. A toilet stub 4 inches off means breaking the slab later.
- Drain pipes slope the right way and are bedded so they won't move when concrete hits them.
- Every sleeve and conduit the trades need is in. Ask them, and get them to confirm.
- Vapour barrier covers the whole area, laps taped, no tears. Patch any hole.
- Rebar or mesh is up on chairs, not lying on the barrier.
- Photograph all of it. This is an as-built record of what is under the floor.

### Step 8. Hold: slab inspection

**What happens.** Everything stops while you, then Building Control, inspect the slab before the pour.

**Materials and trades.** None; this is a check.

**Words you'll hear.** Slab inspection, hold point.

**Inspection.** Building Control checks the slab preparation against the drawings: reinforcement, cover, edge forms and thickened edges, and what is under the slab. Ask the inspector what they will want to see.

**Typical time (varies).** Your check: a couple of hours. Building Control: per their notice period.

**The super checks.** Checklist 4 (before any concrete pour), items 4.1 to 4.4:
- Reinforcement size, spacing, laps and cover per drawing; on chairs.
- Sleeves, conduits and stub-ups in place.
- Vapour barrier intact.
- Edge forms straight, braced, at the right level.

### Step 9. Pour slab; cure

**What happens.** Concrete is placed over the whole slab, vibrated, struck level with a screed board, floated and troweled to the finish specified. Control joints are cut or formed where the drawings show them. Then it is kept wet to cure.

**Materials and trades.** Ready-mix, often a pump, concrete crew and finishers. Vibrator, screed boards, floats, trowels (hand or power trowel), curing materials.

**Words you'll hear.** Screed, float, trowel, power trowel, finish, control joints, curing, burlap, plastic shrinkage cracks.

**Inspection.** No inspection during the pour. Cylinders are taken if the engineer requires them.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 day to pour. Curing: keep wet for 7 days. Ask the engineer how soon the slab can take the load of block work; block work often starts within days.

**The super checks.** Checklist 4 in full and hold point H5:
- Concrete volume ordered with 5% to 10% extra. Example: 30 x 40 ft x 4 in = 14.8 cu yd, + 7% = order 16.
- Pour starts early in the day in heat.
- Tickets checked; no water added without your OK.
- Vibrated, level, finished as specified.
- Curing starts as soon as the surface can take it, and someone is assigned to keep it wet for 7 days.

### Step 10. Block walls to tie-beam height; vertical bars and lintels at openings; fill cores

**What happens.** The main walls go up, course by course, from the slab to the underside of the tie beam. Openings are left for windows and doors at the sizes on the drawings. Vertical bars go in the cores at corners, at the sides of openings, and along the walls at the spacing drawn. Lintels (beams over openings) are set or formed. Cores with bars are filled solid.

**Materials and trades.** Block crew, steel fixer. Block, mortar, rebar, lintels (precast, or bond beam block with bars, or formed and cast in place, per drawing), concrete for core fill. Scaffolding as the walls get higher.

**Words you'll hear.** Courses, rough openings, jambs (sides of an opening), sill (bottom), head (top), lintel, bearing (how far a lintel sits on the wall each side), cores, cleanouts, lifts, scaffold.

**Inspection.** Not usually a separate Building Control stage; the walls' steel is often seen at the tie beam inspection, but core fill is buried before then. That is why your own checks matter most here.

**Typical time (varies).** 2 to 4 weeks for a house this size.

**The super checks.** Hold points H2 and H3:
- Walls plumb, level and straight; joints full.
- Vertical bars at every position on the drawing, lapped correctly.
- Rough openings measured against the window and door sizes you ordered. Impact windows are made to size with 8 to 16 week lead times. If the openings don't match, you find out when the windows arrive.
- Lintels the size and type drawn, with the bearing shown.
- Cleanouts cleared and closed. Cores filled in lifts, rodded or vibrated. Tap for voids.
- Scaffolding on a firm base, braced, fully planked, with guardrails.

### Step 11. Tie beam (belt course) formwork and rebar; hurricane straps set in place before the pour

**What happens.** The **tie beam** is a reinforced concrete beam cast on top of the walls, all the way round the house. Carpenters build plywood forms on both sides of the top of the wall. The steel fixer places the rebar cage: main bars running the length of the beam, with stirrups (loops) around them, and corner bars and laps so the beam is one continuous ring. Then the **hurricane straps** are set at every truss position, so their bottom ends will be cast into the concrete.

**Materials and trades.** Carpenter (formwork), steel fixer, block crew. Plywood and lumber for forms, braces, release oil, rebar, stirrups, tie wire, hurricane straps (galvanised; the type on the drawing), the truss layout drawing.

**Words you'll hear.** Tie beam, belt course, ring beam (all the same thing), formwork, shuttering, cage, stirrups, corner bars, straps, truss layout, embedment.

**Inspection.** The tie beam inspection is step 12.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 2 weeks.

**The super checks.** Hold point H4 and checklist item 4.3:
- Get the truss layout drawing from the truss supplier before you set straps. Mark every truss position on the forms.
- One strap at every mark, the type on the drawing, set to the right depth, tied so it can't move. Count straps; count trusses. Equal.
- Main bar sizes and count, stirrup spacing, corner laps per drawing.
- Cover all round.
- Forms braced, clean, oiled; top set level.

This is the single most important check on the house. Straps forgotten before the tie beam pour, then retrofitted badly, is a classic Bahamian failure, and weak roof-to-wall connections are the main cause of roof loss in hurricanes.

### Step 12. Hold: tie beam inspection

**What happens.** Everything stops while you, then Building Control, inspect the tie beam before the pour.

**Materials and trades.** None; this is a check.

**Words you'll hear.** Tie beam inspection, belt course inspection.

**Inspection.** Building Control checks the beam reinforcement, laps, cover, formwork and straps against the drawings.

**Typical time (varies).** Your check: a few hours on a house. Building Control: per their notice period.

**The super checks.** H4 in full. Walk the whole ring. Photograph every corner and a sample of straps with a tape in the shot.

### Step 13. Pour tie beam; cure

**What happens.** Concrete is placed in the tie beam forms, vibrated carefully around the bars and straps, and the top struck level. It is kept wet to cure. Forms come off when the engineer's drawings or instructions allow.

**Materials and trades.** Ready-mix, usually a pump (the beam is high up), concrete crew. Vibrator, curing materials.

**Words you'll hear.** Pump, vibrate, honeycombing, strip (remove forms), cure.

**Inspection.** None during the pour; cylinders if the engineer requires.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 day to pour. Cure 7 days. Form removal per the engineer.

**The super checks.** Checklist 4 and H5. Also:
- Straps don't get knocked over or pushed down during the pour. Have someone watching them.
- Vibrate fully. Honeycombing in a tie beam exposes steel to salt air.
- After stripping the forms, look for honeycombing and report anything significant to the engineer.

### Step 14. Roof framing (trusses or rafters), every strap fixed, bracing

**What happens.** The roof frame goes up. Most houses use **trusses**: factory-made triangle frames delivered to site, lifted onto the tie beam, set at the spacing on the truss drawings, braced, and fixed to the hurricane straps. Some roofs use **rafters** cut and built on site by the carpenter. Either way, each member is strapped to the tie beam.

**Materials and trades.** Carpenter or framing crew. Trusses (lead time 2 to 6 weeks; order early), bracing lumber, nails (the type on the drawing; ring-shank where specified), possibly a crane or a lot of labour to lift.

**Words you'll hear.** Trusses, rafters, ridge, eaves, overhang, fascia, soffit, spacing, bracing, strap, nail count, ring-shank, galvanised.

**Inspection.** Step 15.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 2 weeks.

**The super checks.** Hold point H6:
- Trusses are the ones on the truss drawings, at the spacing drawn.
- Temporary bracing goes up as each truss is set (unbraced trusses can fall like dominoes), and permanent bracing matches the truss drawings.
- Every strap is wrapped and nailed with the right nail type and number. Count them. Under-nailed straps are a common failure.
- Galvanised or stainless connectors and nails near the coast.

### Step 15. Hold: roof framing and strapping inspection

**What happens.** You, then Building Control, inspect the roof frame and every connection before it is covered.

**Words you'll hear.** Roof framing inspection, strapping inspection.

**Inspection.** Building Control checks truss spacing and bracing, strap connections and nailing against the drawings. Some inspectors also check the sheathing nailing at this stage or the next; ask.

**Typical time (varies).** Your check: a few hours. Building Control: per their notice period.

**The super checks.** H6 in full, with photos of the straps. Once sheathing goes on, the top of the trusses is hidden.

### Step 16. Sheathing, underlayment, roofing: dry-in

**What happens.** **Sheathing** (plywood or OSB sheets) is nailed over the trusses to form the roof deck, using the nailing pattern on the drawing (closer at the edges of each sheet, wider in the middle, called the field). **Underlayment** (a waterproof layer) is laid over the deck with laps running so water flows over each joint. Drip edge goes on the edges. Then the roof covering (shingles or metal) is fixed using the manufacturer's high-wind pattern. Once the roof is covered and openings are closed, the house is **dried in**: rain can't get in.

**Materials and trades.** Carpenter (sheathing), roofer. Plywood or OSB, nails, underlayment, drip edge, shingles or metal roofing, fasteners, flashing at walls, pipes and valleys.

**Words you'll hear.** Sheathing, deck, edge and field nailing, shiners (nails that missed the truss), underlayment, laps, drip edge, flashing, valley, hip, ridge, squares (100 sq ft of roofing), high-wind pattern, dry-in.

**Inspection.** Sheathing nailing may be inspected (ask Building Control). Roofing is not always an official stage, which makes your check more important.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 3 weeks.

**The super checks.** Hold points H6 (sheathing nailing) and H7:
- Sheathing nailing at edges and field matches the drawing. Look underneath for shiners; a nail that missed the truss holds nothing.
- Underlayment laps per the manufacturer.
- Drip edge at all edges.
- Fasteners per the high-wind instructions. Get the instructions before the roofer starts and lift a few shingles to count.
- Schedule dry-in before August if you can. An open roof in hurricane season is your biggest risk.

### Step 17. Windows and exterior doors with flashing and anchoring

**What happens.** Windows and exterior doors are installed in the rough openings. In The Bahamas they are impact-rated, or protected by approved shutters. Each unit is anchored into the block or concrete exactly as its product approval says, flashed so water is sent out at the head and sill, and sealed to the wall.

**Materials and trades.** Window and door installer (often the supplier's crew). Windows and doors (lead time 8 to 16 weeks for impact-rated; order the week the deposit clears), anchors, flashing, sealant, shims.

**Words you'll hear.** Impact-rated, product approval, rough opening, anchors, embedment, shims, flashing, sill pan, head flashing, sealant, operate.

**Inspection.** Building Control may check the products and installation against the approval (ask). Your own check is essential.

**Typical time (varies).** 3 to 7 days to install. The lead time is the real schedule driver.

**The super checks.** Hold point H8:
- The product approval document is on file and covers each size installed.
- Anchors: type, embedment and spacing per the approval. Count them on several units.
- Flashing at heads and sills, before stucco covers it. No flashing at windows is one of the commonest leaks here.
- Sealant continuous and UV-rated.
- Every unit opens, closes and locks.

### Step 18. Electrical, plumbing and AC rough-in by licensed trades

**What happens.** With the house dried in, the trades run everything that will be hidden in walls and ceilings. The electrician installs boxes, conduit and wire, and the panel location. The plumber runs water supply lines and drain and vent pipes in the walls. The AC technician runs ducts, refrigerant line sets and condensate drains. In block walls, the trades cut chases (grooves) in the block for pipes and conduit, or run them in the cores, per the drawings.

**Materials and trades.** Licensed electrician, licensed plumber, AC technician. Boxes, conduit, wire, panel, pipes and fittings, ducts, line sets.

**Words you'll hear.** Rough-in, boxes, conduit, home runs, panel, supply lines, drains, vents, stub-outs, chases, ducts, line sets, condensate drain.

**Inspection.** Step 19.

**Typical time (varies).** 2 to 4 weeks; the trades overlap.

**The super checks.** Hold point H10:
- Every electrician and plumber is licensed (checklist item 2.4).
- Outlet, switch and fixture positions match the drawings and the client's selections. Walk the house with the drawings and, ideally, the client.
- Chases in block walls don't cut through the vertical bars or the filled cores. If a pipe must cross structure, ask the engineer.
- Tell the plaster and drywall subs in writing: no wall closes until the rough-in has passed.

### Step 19. Hold: rough-in inspections

**What happens.** Building Control (and any other inspectors the permit requires) check the electrical and plumbing rough-in before walls and ceilings are covered.

**Words you'll hear.** Electrical rough, plumbing rough, pressure test.

**Inspection.** Electrical: wiring, boxes, panel location, grounding, ground-fault protection where required. Plumbing: pipe sizes and materials, slopes on drains, venting, and often a pressure or water test showing no leaks. Ask your trades what the inspector will want to see.

**Typical time (varies).** Your check plus Building Control's notice period.

**The super checks.**
- Your trades have finished and tested their own work before the inspector comes.
- Photograph every wall and ceiling with the services visible and a tape measure in the shot. These photos are your as-built.
- Nothing is covered until it passes.

### Step 20. Stucco and plaster inside and out; ceilings

**What happens.** The block walls get their skin. Outside, **stucco** (also called render): a cement and washed-sand plaster applied in coats, commonly two or three, to a smooth or textured finish. Inside, plaster on the block walls. Ceilings go up: typically drywall screwed to the underside of the trusses or to furring strips, then taped and finished.

**Materials and trades.** Plasterer, drywall and ceiling installer, labourers. Cement, washed sand (never beach sand), bonding agent if specified, corner beads, drywall, screws, tape, joint compound. Scaffolding outside.

**Words you'll hear.** Stucco, render, scratch coat, brown coat, finish coat, texture, corner bead, drywall, sheetrock, tape and mud, furring.

**Inspection.** Not usually a Building Control stage. Your check.

**Typical time (varies).** 3 to 6 weeks for inside and out.

**The super checks.**
- Rough-in inspections passed before anything is covered.
- Window flashing is in before outside stucco covers it.
- Washed sand only. Check the delivery.
- Stucco kept damp between coats in the sun, as the plasterer's method requires. Stucco that dries too fast cracks, and cracked stucco lets water into the block.
- Walls flat and corners straight. Shine a light along the wall at a low angle to see waves.
- Ceilings: screws at the spacing the board maker requires; joints taped and smooth. Moisture-resistant board in bathrooms and other wet areas.

### Step 21. Waterproofing wet areas; tile

**What happens.** In showers and other wet areas, a **waterproof membrane** goes on the walls and floor, then a **flood test**: plug the drain, fill with water, leave it 24 hours, check for leaks. Then tile: floors throughout (often), walls in bathrooms and kitchen backsplashes, set in thinset, then grouted and sealed.

**Materials and trades.** Tile setter (sometimes a separate waterproofing sub). Membrane, thinset, tile, grout, sealer, spacers, levelling clips.

**Words you'll hear.** Membrane, flood test, falls to drain, thinset, grout, lippage (edges of tiles not flush), hollow tiles, layout, spacers, movement joints.

**Inspection.** Not usually a Building Control stage. Your flood test is the inspection that matters.

**Typical time (varies).** 2 to 4 weeks for a house.

**The super checks.** Hold point H9:
- Membrane covers everything specified, with corners and laps done per the manufacturer.
- Flood test 24 hours before tile, recorded with photos at the start and end.
- Shower floors fall to the drain.
- Tile layout agreed with the client before setting starts (where cuts land, pattern direction).
- Finished tile flat, joints even, no lippage. Tap tiles with a coin; a hollow sound means the tile isn't bedded fully and will crack.

### Step 22. Cabinets, doors, trim

**What happens.** The carpenter and cabinet installer fit kitchen and bathroom cabinets and countertops, interior doors, door frames, baseboards and other trim.

**Materials and trades.** Carpenter, cabinet installer, countertop fabricator. Cabinets (custom lead time 4 to 10 weeks), countertops, doors, frames, hardware, trim, fasteners, sealant.

**Words you'll hear.** Casework, carcass, countertop, backsplash, scribe (cut trim to fit an uneven wall), hinges, hardware, baseboard, casing, reveal.

**Inspection.** Not a Building Control stage. Your check.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 3 weeks.

**The super checks.**
- Cabinets level and fixed firmly to the wall. Doors and drawers line up and close.
- Countertop templates taken after cabinets are fixed, not before.
- Interior doors swing freely, latch, and have even gaps.
- Wood trim kept off the floor slab where water might sit, and treated or sealed as specified. Termites and damp find untreated wood.

### Step 23. Paint

**What happens.** Walls, ceilings, trim and doors are primed and painted. Outside, the stucco is primed and painted with exterior paint rated for sun and salt.

**Materials and trades.** Painter. Primer, paint (interior and exterior), caulk, tape, rollers, brushes, sprayers, drop cloths.

**Words you'll hear.** Primer, coats, coverage, cut in, caulk, sheen (flat, eggshell, semi-gloss), touch-up, colour code.

**Inspection.** Not a Building Control stage. Your check.

**Typical time (varies).** 2 to 3 weeks.

**The super checks.**
- New stucco and plaster have dried as long as the paint maker requires before painting.
- Paint quantity worked out: area / coverage per gallon x coats. Example: 3,200 sq ft of wall / 350 sq ft per gallon x 2 coats = 18.3, so 19 gallons, plus primer separately.
- Colours match the client's written selections. Write down brand, colour code and sheen for each room for the handover folder.
- Clean lines at ceilings and trim. No drips or runs. Floors, windows and fixtures protected.

### Step 24. Fixtures, devices, AC equipment, appliances

**What happens.** The finishing trades come back. The electrician fits devices (switches, outlets) and light fixtures and makes up the panel. The plumber sets toilets, sinks, taps, showers and the water heater. The AC technician sets the units, connects them and starts them up. Appliances go in.

**Materials and trades.** Licensed electrician, licensed plumber, AC technician, appliance installer. Devices, fixtures, AC units (lead time 2 to 8 weeks, longer before hurricane season), appliances.

**Words you'll hear.** Trim-out, devices, fixtures, cover plates, panel schedule, water heater, AC startup, condensate, GFCI outlets.

**Inspection.** Leads into the final inspections at step 25.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 2 weeks.

**The super checks.**
- Every outlet and switch works. Test with a plug-in tester. GFCI outlets where required trip and reset.
- The panel is labelled: which breaker feeds what.
- Every tap, toilet and drain works with no leaks. Run water and look underneath.
- The AC cools, drains properly, and the condensate goes where it should, not onto a wall.
- Collect every manual and warranty as each item goes in (checklist item 6.4).

### Step 25. Final inspections; certificate of occupancy; BPL and Water & Sewerage connections

**What happens.** Building Control carries out the final inspections the permit requires. When everything passes, the **certificate of occupancy** is issued. With it and the utility sign-offs, BPL connects permanent power and Water & Sewerage connects water (and sewer where available).

**Materials and trades.** You, the electrician and plumber for any corrections; BPL and Water & Sewerage crews.

**Words you'll hear.** Final inspection, certificate of occupancy (CO), sign-off, meter, connection, deposit.

**Inspection.** The final inspection looks at the finished building against the approved plans and code. Ask Building Control early in the job what the final covers and which documents to have ready.

**Typical time (varies).** 2 to 6 weeks from booking the final to having power and water. This varies a lot.

**The super checks.**
- Every earlier inspection passed and recorded. A missing record at this stage costs weeks.
- Agreed in writing early on who applies to each utility (normally the owner) and who pays the fees (normally the owner; it is in your exclusions).
- A copy of the certificate of occupancy goes in the job file and your licensing file.

### Step 26. Punch list, final clean, handover

**What happens.** You walk the house yourself and fix what you find. Then you walk it with the client and write the **punch list**: every small item still to fix or finish. You fix them, walk again, and sign off each item. The house gets a final clean. You hand over keys, remotes, codes, warranties, manuals, the paint and product list, and the as-built notes and inside-wall photos. You send the final invoice.

**Materials and trades.** You, any trades with punch items, a cleaner.

**Words you'll hear.** Punch list, snag list, practical completion, handover, defects period, retention.

**Inspection.** The client's walk is the inspection that decides your final payment.

**Typical time (varies).** 1 to 2 weeks. Your template gives 10 working days for punch items.

**The super checks.** The closeout checklists (6 and C1 to C11 in [`02-checklists-explained.md`](02-checklists-explained.md)):
- Punch list signed off item by item.
- Final clean you would photograph for your portfolio.
- Handover folder and keys signed for.
- Final photos taken from the same spots as the "before" photos, filed in your licensing file.

## Part 6. The whole sequence at a glance

Typical, varies. Simple single-storey house, about 1,200 to 1,500 sq ft, New Providence.

| Step | Work | Typical on-site time | Official inspection? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Permit; set out | Permit: older benchmark about 120 days. Set out: 1 to 2 days | No (permit is the approval) |
| 2 | Excavate footings | 1 to 3 days | No |
| 3 | Footing rebar; **hold** | Hours, plus Building Control notice | **Yes: footings** |
| 4 | Pour footings | 1 day, then cure | No |
| 5 | Stem walls; fill cores | 3 to 6 days | Usually no (check permit) |
| 6 | Backfill, compact; termite treatment | 2 to 5 days | Usually no |
| 7 | Under-slab services; vapour barrier; slab steel | 3 to 6 days | Ask about plumbing test |
| 8 | **Hold**: slab | Hours, plus notice | **Yes: slab** |
| 9 | Pour slab; cure | 1 day; keep wet 7 days | No |
| 10 | Block walls to tie beam; lintels; fill cores | 2 to 4 weeks | Usually no |
| 11 | Tie beam forms, steel, straps | 1 to 2 weeks | No |
| 12 | **Hold**: tie beam | Hours, plus notice | **Yes: tie beam** |
| 13 | Pour tie beam; cure | 1 day; keep wet 7 days | No |
| 14 | Roof framing and straps | 1 to 2 weeks | No |
| 15 | **Hold**: roof framing and strapping | Hours, plus notice | **Yes: roof framing** |
| 16 | Sheathing, underlayment, roofing: dry-in | 1 to 3 weeks | Ask |
| 17 | Windows and doors | 3 to 7 days (lead time 8 to 16 weeks) | Ask |
| 18 | MEP rough-in | 2 to 4 weeks | No |
| 19 | **Hold**: rough-in | Hours, plus notice | **Yes: electrical and plumbing rough** |
| 20 | Stucco, plaster, ceilings | 3 to 6 weeks | No |
| 21 | Waterproofing; tile | 2 to 4 weeks | No (your flood test) |
| 22 | Cabinets, doors, trim | 1 to 3 weeks | No |
| 23 | Paint | 2 to 3 weeks | No |
| 24 | Fixtures, devices, AC, appliances | 1 to 2 weeks | No |
| 25 | **Finals**; certificate of occupancy; utilities | 2 to 6 weeks | **Yes: final** |
| 26 | Punch list, clean, handover | 1 to 2 weeks | Client walk |

Add the steps up and allow for overlaps and inspection waits: a house like this typically takes about 6 to 12 months on site after the permit. Weather, hurricanes, late selections, slow inspections and long-lead items push it longer.

**What controls the finish date (the critical path).** Permit, then footings, slab, walls, tie beam, roof and dry-in, then rough-in and its inspection, then finishes, then finals. Long-lead items feed into it: trusses must arrive before step 14, windows before step 17, cabinets before step 22, AC before step 24. Order them the week the deposit clears.

## Part 7. Renovation work: what changes in an existing house

Most of your first jobs will be renovations and repairs, not new houses. The trades and materials are the same. Four things change.

### 1. You open things up and find what nobody could see

When you take off tile, drywall or a ceiling, you find the real condition. In Bahamian houses the common finds are:

| What you find | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| Termite damage in wood: wall plates, door frames, roof framing, cabinets | Wood touching soil or block, untreated lumber, old leaks |
| Rot in wood and soft subfloors | Roof or window leaks, leaking showers and pipes |
| Rust stains, cracked or spalling concrete, exposed rusted rebar | Low cover, beach sand in old concrete, salt air, cracks letting water in |
| Old or unsafe wiring | Age, DIY work, earlier unlicensed electricians |
| Corroded or leaking pipes | Age, poor materials |
| Mould behind shower walls and in closets | Missing waterproofing, poor ventilation, leaks |
| Possible asbestos or lead paint in older buildings | Materials used decades ago |

**How you handle it.**
1. **Before you price:** put hidden damage in your exclusions and the unforeseen conditions clause in your contract (template clause 8). Use 10% to 15% contingency on renovations, for your own small surprises.
2. **When you find it:** stop work on that part. Photograph it with a tape measure in the shot. Tell the client the same day, in writing.
3. **Price it as a change order** and get it signed before you fix it. For repairs where you can't see the extent until you open more, use time and materials with a cap ("not to exceed B$[ ] without written approval").
4. **Emergency only:** if it is unsafe or water is coming in, do the minimum to make it safe, notify the same day, and price within 48 hours (template clause 7.2).
5. **Hazardous materials:** if you suspect asbestos or lead paint, stop and tell the client to bring in a specialist. It is excluded from your price for a reason.

**Example.** Bathroom renovation, contract B$12,000. You remove the tile and find the wall plate behind the tub eaten by termites and the subfloor under it soft. You stop, photograph, message the client, and send a change order: replace 8 ft of plate and 20 sq ft of subfloor, treat for termites, B$850 and 2 extra working days. Client signs. You fix it. New contract price B$12,850, new completion date 2 days later. No argument at the end, because it was agreed before the work.

### 2. Somebody lives there

You are working inside a person's home, often while they live in it. Protect it like it's your mother's.

- **Protect:** floor protection on every path you walk, plastic dust walls with zip doors around the work area, covers on anything that stays. Moving furniture and contents is the owner's job unless you priced it (template clause 6(e)).
- **Clean every day:** sweep, bag debris, remove it to the skip. Dust in the rest of the house is the complaint you'll hear most.
- **Hours and noise:** agree working hours in writing. Warn the client and neighbours before noisy work.
- **Water and power:** tell the client before you shut anything off and for how long. Never leave them without a toilet or water overnight without agreement.
- **Security:** who has keys, which rooms are off limits, valuables put away by the client before you start. A missing ring with your crew in the house is a conversation you never want to have.
- **Safety:** children and pets kept away from the work area. Tools and cords not left in walkways. Nothing sharp or live left out at the end of the day.
- **Insurance:** the owner keeps the existing building and contents insured during the work (template clause 11.2). Your public liability covers damage you cause.

### 3. Permits and structure still apply

- Alterations and additions need a building permit, just as new work does. For cosmetic work, get Building Control's "not required" in writing.
- **Never cut a new opening in a block wall, or remove a wall, without an engineer.** A block wall may be load-bearing, it carries vertical bars in filled cores, and the tie beam above may depend on it. The engineer designs the lintel and any temporary support (shoring) while you work.
- **Additions** tie into the existing house: new footing to old, new slab to old, new walls and tie beam to old, usually with dowels drilled and fixed into the existing concrete. How, where and how deep comes from the engineer's details.

### 4. The sequence is different

A renovation runs like this:

1. Protect the house and the occupants.
2. Make safe: isolate power and water to the work area.
3. Remove and demolish carefully. Keep what the client wants to keep.
4. Inspect what you uncovered. Photograph it.
5. Change orders for hidden damage, signed before you fix.
6. Repair the structure and substrate.
7. Rough-in new services, inspected before covering.
8. Close up, then finishes.
9. Clean, punch list, handover.

**Matching existing finishes** is its own problem. Old tile may be discontinued. Old paint has faded. Tell the client before you start that an exact match may not be possible, and agree in writing what happens if it isn't.

## Part 8. When you go and shadow a real site

Use this guide as your notebook. On every visit, try to see one step in the sequence and answer:

1. Which step is this? What came before it, and what comes next?
2. Which trades are here, and how many people?
3. What hold point is coming, and what will the inspector look at?
4. Find the drawing sheet for this work. Does what you see match it?
5. Where are the bar sizes, spacing and laps written? Measure one lap.
6. How are they keeping safe: PPE, scaffold, ladders, edges, power cords?
7. What would go wrong here if nobody checked?
8. Ask the super: "What's the most common mistake on this step?" Write down the answer.

Ten site visits asking those questions will teach you more than any book, including this one.
