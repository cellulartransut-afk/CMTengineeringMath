# 01. Glossary: every term in plain words

Rev 1 · 2026-11-04

## How to use this

- Terms run A to Z. Look up any word you meet in the consultant files, in a contract, on site or at a government office.
- Each entry says what the term means in plain words, then gives an **Example** or says **Why it matters**.
- Rates, fees and laws come from `consultant/knowledge/02-bahamas-rules.md`, as of 2026-11-03. They change. Each one says who confirms it.
- "See" sends you to another entry.
- Companion guides: [`00-start-here.md`](00-start-here.md), [`02-checklists-explained.md`](02-checklists-explained.md), [`03-how-a-house-gets-built.md`](03-how-a-house-gets-built.md), [`04-setting-up-the-business-explained.md`](04-setting-up-the-business-explained.md), [`05-spreadsheets-explained.md`](05-spreadsheets-explained.md).

Three words used everywhere below:
- **You**: the contractor, your business.
- **Client** or **owner**: the person or company paying you to build.
- **Sub**: a subcontractor, a separate business you hire to do part of the work.

## A

**Access Accelerator.** The government's small business agency. It used to be the Small Business Development Centre (SBDC). It runs grant rounds, loan guarantees with partner banks, and youth programmes.
Why it matters: grants reported up to B$5,000, and 75% to 90% guarantees on bank loans under some programmes (as of 2026-11-03). Rounds open and close. Confirm what is open now with Access Accelerator.

**Accounting software.** A program that records every sale, bill and payment: QuickBooks, Xero, Wave or similar.
Why it matters: without it you will not know which jobs made money until it is too late. Enter every invoice and receipt the week it happens.

**Additional insured.** A person or company added to someone else's insurance policy, so that policy protects them too. Your subcontract requires each sub's public liability policy to name you as additional insured.
Why it matters: if the sub's worker drops a block on the neighbour's car, the sub's insurer protects you as well. Without it, the claim lands on you.

**Advance payment guarantee.** A guarantee from a bank or insurer that protects a client's deposit. If you take the deposit and fail to perform, the client claims on the guarantee.
Why it matters: big clients and government may ask for one before paying a large deposit. You need financial statements and collateral to get it.

**AIA.** American Institute of Architects. Its standard contract forms are used by US developers and architects working here.
Why it matters: they are written for US law. Read every clause and have your attorney check how they work under Bahamian law.

**Allowance.** A sum written into your price for an item the client has not chosen yet: tile, light fittings, cabinets. If the final choice costs more or less, the difference plus your fee is added or taken off by change order.
Example: tile allowance B$4.00 per sq ft supplied, 200 sq ft = B$800. Client picks B$6.50 tile. Extra = 200 x B$2.50 = B$500, plus your agreed fee (say 15% = B$75). Change order = B$575.

**Anchor.** A fastener that fixes something into concrete or block: window anchors, strap anchors, anchor bolts. The drawings or the product approval give the type and spacing.
Why it matters: a hurricane-rated window held in with the wrong anchors, or too few, is no longer hurricane-rated.

**Arbitration.** A private trial. Both sides agree in the contract that a neutral arbitrator, not a court, decides any dispute. The Bahamas has an Arbitration Act (2009).
Why it matters: usually faster and private. Your owner contract template offers arbitration in Nassau for larger jobs. For small debts, a demand letter from an attorney often works first.

**As-built.** A record of what was actually built: where it differs from the drawings, and where things hidden in walls, slabs and ceilings really are.
Example: a photo of the bathroom wall before drywall, showing the pipe runs, with a tape measure in the shot. Five years later the owner hangs a cabinet; your photo stops a drill going through a pipe.

**ASCE 7.** A US engineering standard for loads on buildings, including wind. The Bahamas Building Code 3rd edition was patterned on it.
Why it matters: you will see it on engineers' drawings. It is the engineer's tool, not yours.

**Attorney.** A Bahamian lawyer. Forms companies, reviews contracts, writes demand letters, goes to court.
Why it matters: have one review your owner contract and subcontract templates once before first use.

**Audited and reviewed financial statements.** Your accounts checked by an independent accountant. A review is a lighter check. An audit is a full check.
Why it matters: banks and bond providers want reviewed statements by about year 2 or 3. The Business Licence Act requires audited statements above B$5M turnover (confirm with the Department of Inland Revenue).

## B

**B$.** The Bahamian dollar. Pegged 1 to 1 to the US dollar: B$100 = US$100. Florida prices convert straight across, before freight, duty and VAT.

**Backcharge.** A cost you deduct from what you owe a sub because you had to do something they were supposed to do: clean up, fix a defect, replace damaged work. Your subcontract: written notice, 48 hours to fix (immediately for safety), then you fix it and deduct the cost plus 10%.
Example: the painter leaves paint on new tile and ignores your notice. You pay a cleaner B$200. You deduct B$200 + 10% = B$220 from the painter's next payment. Keep the notice and the photos.

**Backfill.** Soil or fill placed back against foundations, or inside the stem walls to build up to slab level. Compacted in layers.
Why it matters: loose fill settles. A slab poured on loose fill cracks and sinks.

**Backlog.** Signed work you have not done yet.
Why it matters: 2 to 3 months of backlog is healthy. Zero backlog means no income next month.

**Bahamas Building Code.** The minimum rules a building must meet for structure and safety. The 3rd edition (2003) with amendments governs private work. A 4th edition was pending at last report. The design wind speed is widely cited as 150 mph.
Why it matters: your work is inspected against it. Get a copy from the Ministry of Public Works and keep it in the truck.

**Bahamas Development Bank (BDB).** A government development bank that lends to small businesses.
Why it matters: concessional loans reported at 0% to 2% for the first two years, up to about B$100,000, for small businesses including youth-owned ones (2024 programme, as of 2026-11-03). Confirm current terms with BDB.

**Bahamian Contractors Association (BCA).** The trade association for Bahamian contractors. Under the planned Construction Contractors Board it nominates six of the ten members.
Why it matters: credibility with clients, a network of contractors, and early news on licensing. Join in Stage 0.

**Bar chart schedule (Gantt chart).** A schedule drawn as a list of tasks down the left and a calendar across the top. Each task is a bar from its start to its finish.
Example: "Block walls" runs week 5 to week 8; "Tie beam" runs week 8 to week 9. Clients and subs read it in seconds. See also **CPM**.

**Bar size.** The thickness of a reinforcing bar. US-style bars are numbered in eighths of an inch: a #4 bar is 4/8 = 1/2 inch, a #5 bar is 5/8 inch. Some drawings use millimetres (12 mm, 16 mm).
Why it matters: a #4 bar where the drawing says #5 has about a third less steel. Check the size marked on the bar against the drawing.

**Barge / landing craft.** A flat boat chartered to carry big loads (trucks, machines, a whole house of materials) to a Family Island, often landing at a ramp or beach.
Why it matters: you pay for the whole trip, weather delays included. Price it as its own line.

**Batter boards.** Temporary timber frames set a few feet outside each corner of the building. String lines run between them to mark the wall lines.
Why it matters: they keep your lines in place after the corner pegs are dug out during excavation.

**Benchmark (datum).** A fixed point of known height on or near the site that every level on the job is measured from.
Why it matters: if your benchmark gets knocked or moved, every height on the job is wrong.

**Bench (sub bench).** Your list of subs you trust, ideally two per trade, with their documents on file. Kept in `consultant/my-business/subs.md`.

**Bid / quote / tender / proposal.** Words for the price you offer to do a job. "Tender" is used for formal and government bids. Your template is `consultant/templates/quote-proposal.md`.

**Bid bond.** A guarantee from a bank or insurer, given with a formal bid, that you will sign the contract if you win. If you win and walk away, the client claims on it.
Why it matters: government and large private tenders may ask for one. You cannot get one without financial statements and a track record, so start building those in year 1.

**Bid leveling.** Putting sub quotes side by side so you compare the same scope. Quotes never include the same things.
Example: two tile quotes for one bathroom.

| | Tile sub A | Tile sub B |
|---|---|---|
| Quote | B$2,800 | B$3,400 |
| Thinset and grout included? | No, add B$300 | Yes |
| Waterproof membrane included? | No, add B$350 | Yes |
| Removes old tile? | No, add B$250 | Yes |
| **Leveled price** | **B$3,700** | **B$3,400** |

The "cheap" quote is B$300 dearer once leveled. Always level before you choose.

**Block (CMU).** Concrete masonry unit: a hollow concrete block. The standard wall block is 8 x 8 x 16 inches (nominal; the real block is about 3/8 inch smaller to leave room for the mortar joint). Smaller blocks are used where the drawings call for them.
Example: 1.125 blocks per sq ft of wall. Wall 40 ft long x 9 ft high, one 3 x 7 ft door: (360 - 21) x 1.125 = 381 blocks, + 5% waste = 400 blocks.

**Bond beam.** A course of U-shaped "bond beam" blocks laid in the wall, with rebar in the channel, filled solid with concrete or grout. It acts as a beam inside the wall.
Why it matters: people sometimes say "bond beam" when they mean a tie beam. A tie beam is cast in timber formwork; a bond beam is cast inside special blocks. The engineer's drawings say which you are building.

**Boundary pegs (pins).** Markers a land surveyor sets at the corners of a property.
Why it matters: setbacks are measured from them. Guessing the boundary has put houses on neighbours' land.

**BPL.** Bahamas Power and Light, the main electricity utility in New Providence and many Family Islands. (Grand Bahama has its own power company.) Permanent power is connected after final inspections and sign-offs.
Why it matters: connection, meter and deposit fees are normally the owner's cost. Put them in your exclusions.

**Bracing.** Diagonal or cross pieces that stop something from leaning, racking or falling over: trusses, formwork, scaffolds, new walls.
Why it matters: unbraced trusses can fall like dominoes. The truss drawings show the bracing.

**Break-even.** The revenue you need just to pay your overhead, with nothing left over.
Formula: break-even revenue = monthly overhead / gross margin %.
Example: overhead B$2,000 a month, margin 22%: 2,000 / 0.22 = B$9,091 of work every month just to stand still.

**Breakage and loss allowance.** An amount added to landed material cost for items broken or lost in shipping and handling: 2% to 5%, more for Family Islands.

**Building Control.** The section of the Ministry of Public Works that reviews plans, issues building permits and inspects work at stages. In the Freeport area, the Grand Bahama Port Authority does this.
Why it matters: no permit, no start. Failed inspection, no pour. See **Town Planning / Building Control**.

**Building permit.** Written permission from Building Control to build what is on the approved plans. Required for new construction, additions and alterations, including decks, sheds, retaining walls and fences.
Why it matters: work without a permit can be stopped, and the client may not get power connected. An older benchmark put a permit at about 120 days. Complete drawings and same-day answers to queries speed it up.

**Business licence.** The licence every business needs to trade. Issued and renewed by the Department of Inland Revenue under the Business Licence Act, 2023. The tax is on last year's gross turnover, not profit.
Example: reported bands: up to B$100,000 exempt from the tax (licence still required); above B$100,000 to B$500,000, 0.5%; above B$500,000 to B$5M, 0.75%; above B$5M, 1.25%. Confirm with Inland Revenue how the bands apply.

**Buyout.** The step right after the client signs, when you "buy" the job: sign every subcontract and place every purchase order, locking in your real costs.
Example: you estimated plumbing at B$6,000. At buyout you sign the plumber at B$5,600: B$400 saved. If he signs at B$6,500 you are B$500 down before you start, and you know it now, not at the end.

## C

**Callback.** The client calls you back to fix something after you finished.
Why it matters: each callback costs time and money with no new income. Track them. They should fall every year. A fast, polite fix makes a client for life.

**CAR insurance (contractor's all risks).** Insurance on the works while they are being built: fire, theft, storm damage to what you have built and the materials on site. Bought per job or as an annual policy.
Why it matters: if a storm takes the half-built roof, public liability does not pay; CAR does. Check that named storms are covered and what the deductible is.

**Cash flow / cash position.** Cash flow is money in and out over time. Cash position is how much you are ahead at a given point: money in minus money out.
Example: B$40,000 job. Deposit in B$16,000. Spent B$12,000 on materials and setup. Cash position +B$4,000. If it ever goes negative, you are lending the client your money.

**Ceiling (NIB).** The highest wage NIB contributions are charged on: B$810 a week (B$3,510 a month) as of 2026-11-03.
Example: worker earns B$1,000 a week. NIB is charged on B$810 only. Employer 6.65% = B$53.87; employee 4.65% = B$37.67. New rates were expected from 1 July 2026; confirm with NIB.

**Certificate of insurance.** A one-page document from an insurer or broker proving a policy exists: who is insured, what cover, limits, dates, and any additional insured.
Why it matters: you collect one from every sub before they start. Check the dates. An expired certificate is worth nothing.

**Certificate of occupancy (CO).** The document issued after final inspections confirming the building is fit to occupy. Needed, with utility sign-offs, before power and water are connected.
Why it matters: the client cannot move in properly without it, and your final payment usually depends on it.

**Chairs.** Small supports that hold rebar up off the ground or the formwork so concrete flows under and around the steel. Plastic or wire chairs, or small concrete blocks ("dobies").
Why it matters: rebar lying on the dirt has no cover underneath. Water and salt reach it, it rusts, swells and cracks the concrete. Stones and broken block are not chairs.

**Change order (CO).** A signed document that changes the scope, price or time of the contract. Written and signed before the changed work starts. Template: `consultant/templates/change-order.md`. Same thing as a **variation**.
Example: client asks for an extra window. Change order: description, B$1,450, adds 3 working days, both sign, then you cut the opening. No signature, no work.

**Change order log.** A list of every change order: number, date, description, price, days added, signed or pending.
Why it matters: at the end, the log shows the client exactly why the final price is higher than the contract price.

**CIF (cost, insurance and freight).** The value of imported goods at the Bahamian port: supplier price + freight + marine insurance. Customs duty is calculated on it.
Example: FOB B$10,000 + freight B$1,200 + insurance B$100 = CIF B$11,300.

**Cistern.** A tank that stores rainwater from the roof for household use. Common on the Family Islands.

**Cleanout.** An opening cut in the face of the bottom block of a core that will be filled. You look through it to clear out mortar droppings and check the bar is in place, then close it before filling.
Why it matters: mortar droppings piled at the bottom of a core stop concrete reaching the bottom. You get a hollow core, and a weak wall exactly where it needs strength.

**Closeout.** The end stage of a job: punch list, final inspections, certificate of occupancy, handover documents, final payment, sub payments, lessons logged.

**CMU.** See **block**.

**Committed cost.** Money you have agreed to spend through signed subcontracts and purchase orders, even though you have not paid it yet.
Example: plumbing budget B$6,000. Signed subcontract B$5,600 (committed). Paid so far B$2,000 (spent). Left uncommitted: B$400. Watch committed, not just spent, or you overspend without seeing it.

**Compaction.** Pressing soil or fill down with a plate compactor ("whacker") or roller, in layers, so it will not settle later. Layer thickness comes from the drawings or the engineer.
Why it matters: you cannot compact a deep pile of fill from the top. Thin layers are the whole point.

**Company limited by shares.** A company registered under the Companies Act. It is a separate legal person from you, so most business debts and claims are against the company, not your personal things. Banks will still want your personal guarantee on loans.
Why it matters: recommended before any job over about B$25,000, before hiring employees, and before government work.

**Concession (duty concession).** A legal right some clients have to pay less or no customs duty on certain imports: first-time homeowner relief, hotel or resort encouragement acts, Freeport Port Authority licensees, Family Island development concessions.
Why it matters: it can make materials much cheaper, but the paperwork must be in the client's name and done right. Always ask the client if they have one.

**Conduit.** Pipe that carries electrical wires inside slabs, walls and ceilings.

**Consolidator.** A Florida shipping company that combines small shipments from many customers into one container. Lets you ship less than a full container (see **LCL**).

**Construction Contractors Act and Board.** The 2016 law setting up a Board to license contractors and tradespeople by category and size of work. As of April 2026 reporting, the Board had not been appointed, so nobody is licensed under the Act yet. Proposed requirements: proof of a completed project in your category, a valid business licence, no outstanding complaints.
Why it matters: keep a licensing file for every job from job one: contract, permit, photos, invoice, proof of payment, client letter. Confirm status with the Ministry of Public Works and the BCA.

**Contingency.** An amount in your estimate for things you cannot see or predict. 3% to 5% for clear scope; 10% to 15% for renovation with unknowns.
Example: renovation direct cost B$20,000 x 10% = B$2,000. It is not extra profit. It is there for problems that are yours to absorb, not the client's.

**Contractor / general contractor (GC).** The business that holds the contract with the client and is responsible for the whole job. A general contractor usually self-performs some work and manages subs for the rest. A paper contractor subs out all of it.

**Conveyance.** The legal document that transfers ownership of land from one person to another. It shows who owns the property.
Why it matters: on new builds, ask to see it (or the survey). The person signing your contract should own the land or have written authority from the owner.

**Core fill / grout.** Concrete or grout poured into the hollow cores of a block wall where rebar is placed. Each filled core becomes a small reinforced concrete column inside the wall.
Why it matters: this is what holds the wall in a hurricane. Hollow or half-filled cores are one of the commonest failures in The Bahamas.

**Cost book.** Your own record of what things really cost: item, unit, real cost, date, supplier, island.
Why it matters: after 10 jobs your estimates come from your own data, not guesses. The biggest single estimating upgrade there is.

**Cost code.** A number for a type of cost, so every bill on a job lands in the right bucket. Examples from `06-money.md`: 01 General conditions, 03 Concrete, 04 Masonry, 06 Carpentry, 09 Finishes, 15 Plumbing, 16 Electrical. Full list: `consultant/knowledge/07-cost-codes.md`.
Example: the block delivery invoice is coded 04. At month end you see masonry spent B$7,200 against a B$6,500 budget, and you know exactly where money leaked.

**Cost plus (cost plus fee).** A contract where the client pays your actual cost plus an agreed fee, fixed or a percentage. Open books: you must prove every cost.
Example: cost B$50,000 + 15% fee = B$57,500. Low risk to you, but only for trusted clients.

**Cost to complete (forecast to finish).** What you expect it will still cost to finish a cost code or the whole job.
Example: concrete budget B$12,000, spent B$9,000, one pour left estimated at B$4,000. Forecast final B$13,000: B$1,000 over. You know now and can act.

**Course.** One horizontal row of blocks in a wall.

**Cover (concrete cover).** The thickness of concrete between the rebar and the outside face of the concrete.
Why it matters: cover protects the steel from salt and water. Too little and the steel rusts, swells and breaks the concrete off (spalling). The hold point table calls for 3 inches of cover in footings. Other members: per the engineer's drawings.

**CPM (critical path method).** A way of scheduling where each task is linked to the tasks that must finish before it can start. The longest chain of linked tasks is the **critical path**. It sets the finish date.
Example: slab, then walls, then tie beam, then roof, then dry-in. A week lost on any of those is a week lost on the job. A week lost painting the boundary wall may not be, if there is slack.

**Crew list.** A list of every person a sub will bring to site, with their status: Bahamian, permanent resident, or work permit holder.
Why it matters: it is how you check nobody on your site is working illegally.

**Critical path.** See **CPM**.

**Cubic yard (cu yd).** The unit concrete is ordered in. 1 cubic yard = 27 cubic feet (3 ft x 3 ft x 3 ft).
Example: slab 20 ft x 30 ft x 4 in. 20 x 30 x 0.333 = 200 cu ft. 200 / 27 = 7.4 cu yd. Add 7% waste = 7.9. Order 8 cu yd.

**Cure (curing).** Keeping new concrete wet and protected so it gains full strength. Concrete hardens by a chemical reaction with water, not by drying out.
Why it matters: Bahamian sun dries a slab surface in hours. Dried too fast, it cracks and ends up weaker. Keep it wet for 7 days: wet burlap, sprinkling, or plastic sheeting.

**Customs broker.** A licensed agent who clears your imports through Bahamas Customs: classifies the goods, calculates duty and VAT, files the papers.
Why it matters: wrong classification means delays and penalties. The broker's fee is cheap insurance.

**Cylinders (concrete test cylinders).** Samples of fresh concrete cast into cylinder moulds on the day of a pour, then crushed by a testing lab at set ages (commonly 7 and 28 days) to prove the concrete reached the strength the engineer specified.
Why it matters: if a pour is ever questioned, the cylinders are the proof. Take them wherever the engineer requires.

## D

**Daily report.** A one-page record written every day: weather, who was on site, work done, inspections, deliveries, problems, instructions, safety, photos. Template: `consultant/templates/daily-report.md`.
Why it matters: memory loses arguments; records win them. It is your evidence for time extensions, change orders and licensing.

**Days to get paid (receivables days).** How long, on average, clients take to pay your invoices. Under 30 days for private clients is healthy. Track government separately.

**Deductible (excess).** The part of an insurance claim you pay yourself before the insurer pays.
Example: B$2,500 deductible on B$10,000 of storm damage: you pay B$2,500, the insurer pays B$7,500.

**Defects liability period (defects period).** The time after practical completion during which you must come back and fix defects in your work. Your template sets 12 months.
Why it matters: it defines how long your exposure lasts. Your subs give you the same 12 months, so you can pass the fix to whoever caused it.

**Delivery note (delivery ticket).** The paper that comes with a delivery listing what was sent. For ready-mix concrete the ticket shows the mix, strength, slump, volume, time batched and any water added.
Why it matters: check and count before you sign. Write damage or shortages on the note. Once you sign it clean, the supplier owes you nothing.

**Demobilisation / remobilisation.** Packing up and leaving site (demob). Coming back and setting up again (remob). Both cost money.
Why it matters: your contract lets you charge for them if the client causes a suspension.

**Department of Inland Revenue (DIR).** The tax office. Handles business licences and VAT.

**Department of Physical Planning.** The office that handles Town Planning: zoning, use, setbacks, subdivision approval.

**Deposit.** Money the client pays when the contract is signed, before you buy anything. Target 30% to 40%. It must cover materials and mobilisation.
Example: B$10,000 job. First materials B$3,500, setup B$500. You need B$4,000, which is 40%, before you buy anything.

**Design-build.** One contract for both design and construction. You carry the design risk too.
Why it matters: only with a registered architect or engineer working under you and professional indemnity insurance. A later-stage business.

**Design wind speed.** The wind speed a building is designed to survive. Widely cited as 150 mph for The Bahamas under the current code. The engineer uses it to size straps, bars and connections.

**Devices / fixtures.** Devices: switches and outlets. Fixtures: toilets, sinks, taps, showers, light fittings. Fitted near the end, after paint.

**Dewatering.** Pumping water out of an excavation. Excluded from your price unless you priced it.

**Direct cost.** Everything the job itself costs: materials, subs, your own labour, equipment, general conditions, contingency. Does not include overhead.
Example (bathroom): subs B$4,500 + materials B$4,000 + skip and cleanup B$400 + contingency B$445 = B$9,345.

**Direct dealing.** A sub taking instructions, payments or side work from your client on your job. Your subcontract bans it during the job and for 12 months after.
Why it matters: it cuts you out and breaks your control of scope and quality, while you still carry the liability.

**Dobies.** Small concrete blocks used as rebar chairs. See **chairs**.

**Documents and priority (order of precedence).** The contract clause listing the documents that make up the contract (agreement, scope, drawings, specs, proposal) and which one wins if they disagree.
Why it matters: when the drawing says one thing and the scope says another, this clause settles it without a fight.

**Dowels.** Short bars cast into a footing (or slab or beam) and left sticking out so the next wall or pour can lap onto them.
Why it matters: dowels in the wrong place mean the wall bars cannot line up with the block cores. Check positions before the footing pour.

**Drip edge.** A metal strip along the roof edges that throws water clear of the fascia.

**Dry-in.** The point when the building is closed to rain: roof sheathed and covered with underlayment or roofing, openings closed.
Why it matters: until dry-in, rain and storms damage everything inside. Schedule dry-in before August if you can.

**Drywall (gypsum board, sheetrock).** Panels of gypsum between paper faces, screwed to framing for walls and ceilings. A standard sheet is 4 x 8 ft = 32 sq ft.
Example: ceiling 600 sq ft / 32 = 18.75 sheets, + 10% = 20.6. Order 21 sheets.

**Duty (customs duty).** Tax on imported goods, calculated on the CIF value at a rate set item by item in the First Schedule of the Tariff Act.
Why it matters: there is no single "building materials" rate. Check the Bahamas Customs duty calculator or ask a licensed broker before every import estimate.

## E

**Employee vs subcontractor.** If you control how and when someone works, supply the tools and pay by the hour, they are probably your employee, and NIB applies. A true sub has their own business licence, tools and crew, and quotes a price for a scope.
Why it matters: calling an employee a "sub" does not change what they are. You can end up owing NIB and carrying their injuries.

**Employment Act, 2001.** The main law on employment: written terms, hours, overtime, holidays. Confirm details with the Department of Labour.

**Entire agreement.** A clause saying the written contract is the whole deal; earlier talks and promises do not count.
Why it matters: if you promised something in conversation, put it in the document or it is gone. Same for the client.

**eProcurement portal.** The government's online system for registering as a supplier and bidding on government work (Ministry of Finance / Public Procurement Department).

**Escalation clause.** A clause letting you raise the price if a named material's supplier price rises more than a set percentage after signing, through no delay of yours.
Example: threshold 5%. Lumber quoted B$6,000 at signing. At purchase it is B$6,600, up 10%. Price adjusted by the actual increase, B$600, on proof of the supplier's invoices.

**Estimate.** Your prediction of what the job will cost you. Price comes after. Most bad estimates fail by missing items, not by wrong unit prices.

**Excavation.** Digging: footing trenches, septic tanks, soak-aways, levelling. Rock excavation (cutting limestone) is slow and costly. Exclude it unless you priced it.

**Exclusions.** A written list of what your price does not include.
Why it matters: it stops arguments before they start. Standard list in `03-contracts.md`: permit fees, drawings, utility connection fees, rock, hidden damage, hazardous materials, moving furniture, landscaping, owner-supplied items, hurricane damage during construction.

**Extension of time (EOT).** More time added to the completion date for a delay you did not cause: weather and hurricanes, owner changes and late decisions, permit and inspection delays, shipping and customs, unforeseen conditions.
Why it matters: without it you are "late", and the client can hold money or claim delay damages. Your template requires written notice within 7 days of the delay. Send it.

## F

**Falls (to drain).** The slope of a floor toward a drain so water runs away instead of ponding.
Why it matters: a shower floor with no fall holds water, grows mould and leaks.

**Family Islands.** All the inhabited islands except New Providence and Grand Bahama. Work there costs more: mailboat or barge freight, crew travel and housing, food, per diem, double handling, breakage, delays.

**Fascia and soffit.** The fascia is the board along the lower edge of the roof that closes off the ends of the trusses or rafters. The soffit is the underside of the roof overhang.

**FIDIC.** The International Federation of Consulting Engineers. Publishes standard construction contracts used on international, resort and government projects.
Why it matters: you will meet it on larger jobs. Read every amendment; that is where risk gets moved to you.

**Final inspection.** The last inspections by Building Control (and utility inspectors) before the certificate of occupancy.

**First-time homeowner relief.** A VAT rebate on construction services and materials for first-time homeowners (Section 55A of the VAT Act; up to B$40,000 under the 2022 measures). The 2026/27 budget proposed extending relief to duplexes through fourplexes with one owner-occupied unit.
Why it matters: tell qualifying clients. It helps them say yes. Confirm with the Department of Inland Revenue.

**Flashing.** Thin metal or membrane fitted where two surfaces meet (window head and wall, roof and wall, pipes through the roof) to send water out, not in.
Why it matters: no flashing at windows is one of the commonest leaks in Bahamian block houses. Water gets into the block and shows up inside as stains and mould.

**Flood test.** Filling a waterproofed shower or wet area with water and leaving it 24 hours to prove there is no leak, before tile goes on.
Why it matters: finding the leak after tiling means tearing out the tile.

**Flow-down.** A subcontract clause binding the sub to you, for their part of the work, on the same terms you are bound to the client: quality, standards, warranty, schedule, site rules.
Why it matters: if the client holds you to 12 months' warranty and the drawings, you must be able to hold the sub to the same.

**FOB (free on board).** The supplier's price with goods loaded at their dock or port. Everything after that (freight, insurance, duty, VAT, broker, trucking) is your cost.
Why it matters: a Florida FOB price is never your cost. See **landed cost**.

**Footing.** The concrete base, wider than the wall, that spreads the building's weight into firm ground. In a Bahamian block house it is usually a reinforced concrete strip under every load-bearing wall.

**Force majeure.** Events outside anyone's control (hurricanes, war, pandemics, a port shutdown) that excuse a party from delay or performance while they last. It only works if the contract has a clause for it.
Why it matters: your small works template covers this in clause 5.3 (extensions of time for hurricanes and "any other cause outside the Contractor's reasonable control"). Bigger forms have a separate force majeure clause. Read what it covers and whether it gives you time only, or time and money.

**Formwork (forms, shuttering).** Temporary moulds, usually plywood and lumber, that hold wet concrete in shape until it hardens: tie beam sides, columns, slab edges.
Why it matters: weak or unbraced formwork bursts during the pour. You lose the concrete, the time, and sometimes someone gets hurt.

**Freeport / GBPA.** In the Freeport area of Grand Bahama, the Grand Bahama Port Authority licenses businesses and handles building permits (Building and Development Services). To trade in the Port area you need a GBPA business licence as well as the national one.

**Freight.** The cost of shipping goods: Florida to Nassau or Freeport, then mailboat or barge to the Family Islands.

---

## G

**Gate.** In your roadmap, the conditions you must meet before moving to the next stage. Example: "5 jobs completed and paid" before Stage 1.
Why it matters: growing before you pass the gate just grows a bigger failure.

**General conditions.** The costs of running a job that belong to no single trade: supervision time, travel and fuel, temporary toilet, skip bins and dump fees, temporary water and power, scaffolding, small tools and consumables, PPE and first aid, permits (if included), job insurance, security, final clean, protection of finishes, hurricane prep, photos and documents, and on Family Islands crew travel, housing, food, per diem and vehicle hire.
Why it matters: beginners leave these out and lose money on every job without seeing why.

**GFCI / ground-fault protection.** Ground fault circuit interrupter. A device in an outlet, breaker or extension lead that cuts the power in a fraction of a second if electricity leaks to ground, for example through a person.
Why it matters: wet ground and power tools kill people. All temporary power on your site must have ground-fault protection.

**GMP (guaranteed maximum price).** A cost plus contract with a ceiling. The client pays your actual cost plus fee up to the GMP. You pay anything over it. Savings below it are often shared.
Example: GMP B$300,000. Cost plus fee comes to B$310,000: client pays B$300,000 and you eat B$10,000. Comes to B$285,000: client pays B$285,000, and you may share the B$15,000 saving if the contract says so.

**Governing law.** The clause naming which country's law applies to the contract. Yours: the laws of the Commonwealth of The Bahamas.

**Grandfathering.** A transition window when existing contractors can get licensed under new rules based on their track record. The BCA reported it would run about 2026 to 2028, starting when the Board is announced.

**Gross margin.** Gross profit as a percentage of the price.
Formula: gross margin = (price - direct cost) / price.
Example: price B$11,981, direct cost B$9,345. (11,981 - 9,345) / 11,981 = 22%.

**Gross profit.** Price minus direct cost. It pays your overhead and your net profit.
Example: B$11,981 - B$9,345 = B$2,636.

**Gross turnover.** All the money your business takes in from sales in a year, before any costs. The business licence tax is charged on it.

**Grout.** See **core fill / grout**. Tile grout is a different thing: the filler between tiles.

**Guardrail.** A rail at about waist height, with a mid rail, along an open edge (roof, scaffold, slab edge) to stop people falling.

---

## H

**Harness (fall arrest harness).** A body harness clipped to a secure anchor point so a falling worker is caught before hitting the ground.

**Health and Safety at Work Act.** The law that applies to safety on Bahamian work sites. Run the safety system in `05-field-playbook.md`. Confirm details with the Department of Labour.

**Hold point.** A point in the work where everything stops until a check is done and passed: by you, and by Building Control where required. Examples: footing rebar before the pour, tie beam straps before the pour, waterproofing before tile.
Why it matters: once concrete or tile covers it, a mistake is buried. Checking costs minutes. Fixing costs thousands.

**Holding company.** A company that owns other companies. Stage 4 of your roadmap: one company for contracting, one for real estate, one for equipment, so a disaster in one does not take down the others.

**Honeycombing.** Voids and gravel pockets in hardened concrete where it was not vibrated or rodded properly.
Why it matters: weak spots that let water reach the steel.

**Hot-dip galvanised.** Steel dipped in molten zinc to protect it from rust. Used for straps, connectors and fasteners, especially near the coast. Stainless steel is better for exposed hardware.

**Hurricane plan.** The written plan for protecting a job from storms. Every job with work between June 1 and November 30 has one. Key step: site secured 72 hours before expected landfall.

**Hurricane season.** June 1 to November 30.

**Hurricane shutters.** Panels or systems that cover windows and doors in a storm. The 3rd edition code made shutters mandatory; impact-rated openings are the alternative. The product must be approved for the opening.

**Hurricane strap (hurricane clip, tie-down).** A galvanised steel strap cast into the tie beam at every truss or rafter position, then nailed to the truss. It ties the roof down to the walls.
Why it matters: weak roof-to-wall connections are the main cause of roof loss in hurricanes. Straps forgotten before the tie beam pour, then retrofitted badly, is a classic Bahamian failure.

**Hurricane watch / warning.** A watch means hurricane conditions are possible in the area. A warning means they are expected. The Bahamas Department of Meteorology and the US National Hurricane Center issue them and define the time windows.

## I

**Impact-rated.** Windows, doors or shutters tested to survive being hit by flying debris and then high wind pressure. They come with a product approval document showing the rating and how they must be installed and anchored.
Example: lead time for impact windows and doors is 8 to 16 weeks to Nassau. Order them the week the deposit clears.

**Indemnity.** A promise to cover someone else's losses from claims. "The sub indemnifies you" means that if the sub's work or people cause injury or damage and someone claims against you, the sub pays.
Why it matters: watch for contracts asking you to indemnify the client even for the client's own negligence. Unlimited indemnity is a red flag.

**Industrial benefits (NIB).** NIB benefits for employees injured at work: injury benefit (up to 40 weeks, 3 waiting days), medical care, disablement. The employer files form B44 (Employer's Report on Accident). Claims within 3 months. NIB states employers who pay contributions are excused from liability for job injuries and diseases.
Why it matters: it covers your employees who are contributed for. A sub's workers are the sub's responsibility, which is why you need their NIB letter of good standing.

**Input VAT / output VAT.** Output VAT is the VAT you charge on your invoices. Input VAT is the VAT you pay on what you buy for the business (materials, sub invoices). A registered business pays Inland Revenue the difference, subject to the rules.
Example: you invoice B$20,000 + 10% VAT = B$2,000 output VAT. You paid B$900 VAT on materials and subs (input VAT). You owe B$2,000 - B$900 = B$1,100. If you are not registered, you charge no VAT and the B$900 is simply a cost.

**Inspection.** A check of work at a stage. Two kinds: yours (the super's hold point check) and the official one by Building Control or a utility. Typical official stages: footings, slab, tie beam, roof framing and straps, electrical rough, plumbing rough, final.

**Insurance broker.** A licensed agent who gets quotes from insurers for you and places your policy. Get quotes from at least two Bahamian brokers.

**Invoice.** See **payment application**.

## J

**JCT.** The Joint Contracts Tribunal (UK). Publishes standard building contracts used on larger Bahamian, resort and government projects. They fit English-style law, which Bahamian law is built on.
Why it matters: like FIDIC, read every amendment.

**Job costing.** Recording every cost against its job and cost code, then comparing it with the estimate.
Why it matters: it is the only way to know which jobs make money and which kind of work to chase.

## K

**Kickoff meeting.** The first meeting with the client before work starts: who decides, how changes work, selection deadlines, payment dates, how and when you report (weekly update with photos).

## L

**Labourer.** A worker who carries, mixes, digs and cleans. Often the first role on site, and the best way to learn one.

**Landed cost.** What a material really costs you delivered to site: supplier price + freight + insurance + duty + import VAT + broker + port charges + trucking + Family Island freight + breakage allowance.
Example (illustration; the 10% duty rate is assumed only to show the math):

| Line | B$ |
|---|---|
| FOB price, Florida | 10,000 |
| Freight to Nassau | 1,200 |
| Marine insurance | 100 |
| **CIF** | **11,300** |
| Duty, assumed 10% of CIF | 1,130 |
| Import VAT, 10% of (CIF + duty) | 1,243 |
| Broker fee | 250 |
| Port, wharfage, handling | 150 |
| Trucking to site | 200 |
| Breakage allowance, 2% of CIF | 226 |
| **Landed cost** | **14,499** |

The B$10,000 shelf price really costs about B$14,500. Price landed, not shelf. Confirm the duty rate and VAT base with a licensed broker.

**Lap (lap splice).** The length two reinforcing bars overlap where one ends and the next begins, tied together, so the load passes from one to the other through the concrete. The engineer specifies it, often 40 to 48 bar diameters.
Example: #5 bar (5/8 inch) at 48 diameters: 48 x 0.625 = 30 inches of overlap. A 12 inch lap where 30 is drawn is a weak spot.

**LCL (less than container load).** A shipment too small for a whole container, combined with other people's goods by a consolidator.
Why it matters: cheaper than a full container for small orders, but more handling and sometimes slower.

**Lead time.** The time from placing an order to having the item on site.
Example: impact windows 8 to 16 weeks to Nassau; trusses 2 to 6 weeks; Family Islands add 1 to 3 weeks. Order the windows late and the house sits open into hurricane season.

**Letter of good standing (NIB).** A letter from the National Insurance Board confirming a business is up to date with its NIB contributions.
Why it matters: collect one from every sub before they start. You need your own for government tenders and many corporate clients.

**Licensed trades.** Electricians and plumbers must be licensed for their trades, and their work is inspected. You hire them as subs.

**Lien (mechanics' lien), and why The Bahamas has none.** In many countries (most US states, for example) an unpaid contractor can register a legal claim against the property itself, so it cannot easily be sold or refinanced until the contractor is paid. The Bahamas has no mechanics' lien law. A lien bill was discussed in 2017, but as of 2026-11-03 there is no lien law. Contractors cannot put a claim on the house.
Why it matters: you cannot fall back on the property. Your only security is the contract: deposit, milestone billing, right to stop work, title retention, interest and legal costs. If the client still will not pay, you sue or arbitrate. That is why every job needs a written contract and a deposit.

**Lifts.** Stages of height. "Fill cores in lifts" means fill a few feet, rod it, then the next few feet, instead of dumping concrete from the top of a tall wall.

**Lintel.** A beam over a door or window opening that carries the blockwork above. Precast concrete, cast in place, or bond beam blocks with rebar, per the drawings.
Why it matters: a missing or undersized lintel cracks the wall above the opening.

**Liquidated damages (LDs, delay damages).** A fixed amount per day or week the client can deduct if you finish late, agreed in advance in the contract.
Example: B$250 a week, capped at 5% of a B$40,000 price = B$2,000 maximum. Never accept uncapped LDs. Tie them to real loss.

**Load path.** The route wind and weight travel from the roof to the ground: sheathing, trusses, straps, tie beam, vertical bars in filled cores, dowels, footing, soil. Every link must be connected.
Why it matters: a house fails at its weakest link. One missing strap can start a roof peeling off.

**Long-lead item.** Anything with a long lead time: impact windows and doors, trusses, AC units, generators, special fixtures, custom cabinets. Order these first.

**Look-ahead schedule (three-week look-ahead).** A short, detailed schedule of the next three weeks: tasks, which sub, crew size, deliveries, inspections. Updated weekly and sent to subs.
Why it matters: it makes you order, book and call before you need things, not on the day.

**Lump sum (fixed price).** One agreed price for a defined scope. You carry the risk of costs coming in higher.
Why it matters: most of your work will be lump sum. It only works if the scope is written tightly.

## M

**Mailboat.** Scheduled cargo and passenger boats from Potter's Cay in Nassau to the Family Islands.
Why it matters: schedules depend on weather and the boat. Plan for delays, breakage and double handling.

**Margin.** See **gross margin**. Not the same thing as markup.

**Marine insurance.** Insurance on goods while they are being shipped. Part of the CIF value.

**Markup.** The percentage added on top of cost to get the price.
Formulas: margin = markup / (1 + markup). Markup = margin / (1 - margin). Price = cost / (1 - target margin).
Example: to keep a 20% margin you must mark up 25%, not 20%. Cost B$10,000 x 1.25 = B$12,500; margin = 2,500 / 12,500 = 20%. Mark up only 20%: price B$12,000, margin 16.7%. Mixing up the two words cost you B$500.

**Master schedule.** The full job schedule from start to handover. The look-ahead is cut from it.

**Mediation.** A neutral person helps both sides reach a deal. The mediator does not decide. If there is no deal, you go to arbitration or court. Your template: talk first, then mediation in Nassau, then arbitration or the courts.

**MEP.** Mechanical, electrical and plumbing: the building's services. In a house: AC (mechanical), wiring and power (electrical), water, drains and fixtures (plumbing).

**Mesh (welded wire mesh).** Sheets of steel wire welded in a grid, used as slab reinforcement where the drawings allow it. Must sit up on chairs, not lie on the vapour barrier.

**Milestone billing.** Billing the client when a defined stage of work is done ("demolition and rough-in complete"), rather than by the calendar.
Why it matters: you bill ahead of or matching your spending, so you never finance the job.

**Minimum job charge.** The lowest price you will do any job for, because every job takes time to sell, schedule and manage. `06-money.md` example: B$350 to B$500.

**Minimum wage.** B$260 a week, B$6.50 an hour, since January 2023. Confirm no later change with the Department of Labour.

**Mobilisation.** Getting set up on site at the start: moving in people, tools, toilet, skip bin, first materials, protection. It costs money before any work is done, so the deposit must cover it.

**Moisture-resistant board.** Drywall made to resist damp, for bathrooms, kitchens and laundries. It is not waterproof by itself; showers still need a waterproof membrane.

**Mortar.** A mix of cement, sand (sometimes lime) and water used to bed and bond blocks. Joints should be full.

**Mould.** Fungus that grows in damp. Prevented by waterproofing, ventilation, proper AC sizing and drainage.

## N

**Named storm.** A tropical storm or hurricane given a name by forecasters. Many insurance policies treat named storm damage separately, with its own deductible or an exclusion.
Why it matters: "Is named storm covered, and what is the deductible?" is the first question on any CAR policy.

**Near miss.** An incident where nobody got hurt but could have. A block falls off the scaffold and misses a worker.
Why it matters: record it and fix the cause. Next time it hits someone.

**Net profit.** Gross profit minus overhead. What grows the company. Target 8% or more for small work.
Example: revenue B$250,000 at 24% margin = B$60,000 gross profit. Overhead (including your salary) B$40,000. Net profit B$20,000 = 8%.

**Net yield.** Yearly net income from an asset divided by what you invested in it.
Example from `06-money.md`: B$15,120 net rent / B$220,000 invested = 6.9%.

**NIB (National Insurance Board).** The Bahamian social security system. Employers and employees contribute on wages. As of 2026-11-03: employer 6.65%, employee 4.65%, total 11.3% of wages up to B$810 a week. Monthly returns and payments due by the 15th; late penalty reported at 5% a month. Self-employed people contribute too (check your own rate with NIB). New rates were expected from 1 July 2026; confirm with NIB.
Example: employee paid B$500 a week. Employer pays 6.65% = B$33.25. Employee's 4.65% = B$23.25 is deducted from their pay. Total to NIB: B$56.50 for that week.

**No damages for delay.** A clause saying you get extra time for client-caused delay, but no money.
Why it matters: you keep paying crew, equipment and overhead while the client holds you up. Red flag: negotiate it out or price it.

**Notice.** A formal written message under the contract: delay notice, payment demand, defect notice, termination. The contract says how notices are sent and when they count as received. Your template accepts email and WhatsApp.
Why it matters: many rights exist only if you give notice on time and in writing.

## O

**Overhead.** What the business costs whether or not you have a job: phone, vehicle, fuel, insurance, licences, software, accounting, marketing, office, your own salary.
Example: B$2,000 a month = B$24,000 a year. It must be paid out of gross profit.

**Overhead percentage.** Yearly overhead divided by yearly revenue.
Example: B$40,000 / B$250,000 = 16%.

**Overtime.** Reported at 1.5 times pay after 40 hours a week or 8 hours a day; public holidays and rest days at 2 times (Employment Act, 2001). Confirm with the Department of Labour.

**Owner-builder.** A person who builds on their own property and manages it themselves. A planned licensing category under the Act.

**Owner's representative.** Someone the owner pays to watch their contractor and protect their interests. Common for owners abroad building on Family Islands. A future service business in your roadmap.

**Owner-supplied items.** Materials or equipment the client buys and hands you to install.
Why it matters: you warrant the installation, not the item. Late or defective owner items extend your time. Put that in writing.

**OSB (oriented strand board).** Sheets made of wood strands glued under pressure. Used like plywood for roof sheathing where the drawings allow it.

## P

**Paper contractor.** A contractor who holds the contract with the client and manages subs who do all the physical work. You sell management: getting work, pricing, contracts, picking and controlling subs, protecting cash, delivering quality on time.
Why it matters: you carry the liability for every sub's work and safety, even if you never touch a tool.

**Parties.** The people or companies who sign a contract. Use full legal names and addresses. You sue a legal person, not a nickname.

**Pay-when-paid / pay-if-paid.** Pay-when-paid: you pay the sub within a set time after the client pays you for their work. It is about timing. Pay-if-paid: you pay the sub only if the client pays you at all. It moves the risk of a non-paying client onto the sub. The UK outlawed these by statute; The Bahamas has no equivalent statute, and courts read such clauses strictly.
Why it matters: your subcontract uses pay-when-paid with a 30-day backstop when the client withholds for a reason that is not the sub's fault. Have your attorney settle the wording. When someone gives you a contract with pay-if-paid and no time limit, you might never be paid.

**Payback (equipment).** How many months equipment takes to earn back what it cost.
Formula: purchase price / (monthly rental income - monthly running, maintenance and insurance).
Example (illustration): mixer B$4,800, earns B$400 a month in rental, costs B$100 a month to run: 4,800 / 300 = 16 months. Under 24 months with steady demand is a good buy.

**Payment application (pay app).** Your invoice for a stage or period, showing contract value, percent complete, earned to date, previously billed, retention and amount due. Template: `consultant/templates/payment-application.md`.
Example: stage worth B$10,000, 100% done, client holds 5% retention (B$500). Due B$9,500, plus VAT if you are registered.

**Per diem.** A daily allowance paid to workers away from home for food and expenses, mostly on Family Island jobs.

**Performance bond.** A guarantee from a bank or insurer that pays the client, often up to 10% of the contract value, if you fail to finish.
Example: B$400,000 contract, 10% bond = B$40,000. The bank will want cash collateral or a credit facility behind it.

**Permit.** See **building permit**.

**Personal guarantee.** Your personal promise to repay a company debt if the company cannot.
Why it matters: even with a company, banks ask for one on loans. Your personal savings and property are then on the line for that loan.

**Plumb, level, line.** Plumb: perfectly vertical. Level: perfectly horizontal. Line: straight along its length. Checked with a spirit level, plumb bob or laser, and a string line.

**Potter's Cay.** The dock in Nassau, under the Paradise Island bridge, where mailboats load for the Family Islands.

**PPE (personal protective equipment).** Hard hat, safety boots, gloves, eye protection, hearing protection for cutting, harness for work at height.

**Practical completion.** The point when the work is finished except for minor items that do not stop the client using it. It triggers the completion payment, starts the defects period, and starts the punch list.

**Pre-start meeting.** The meeting with each sub before they start: scope, drawings, schedule, crew size, site rules, quality standard, how they get paid.

**Pressure-treated lumber.** Wood treated with chemicals under pressure to resist rot and termites. Use where specified, especially near the ground or against masonry. Even treated, no wood in contact with soil.

**Price gouging.** Raising prices unfairly after a disaster because people are desperate. Don't. Pass on real cost increases with proof, at your normal margin. Your reputation lasts longer than the storm.

**Product approval.** The test document for a window, door, shutter or roofing product showing its wind and impact rating and exactly how it must be installed and anchored. Many products sold from Florida carry Florida or Miami-Dade approvals. Ask Building Control which approvals it accepts.

**Professional indemnity insurance.** Insurance against claims from design mistakes. Needed only if you do design-build.

**Project manager (PM).** The person who runs the contract side of a job: scope, changes, money, schedule, client, documents. The super runs the site. At the start you are both.

**Public liability insurance (general liability).** Insurance against claims for injury or damage to other people caused by your work: the neighbour's car, a passer-by, the client's furniture.
Why it matters: buy it before your first job. Require every sub to carry it and name you on the certificate.

**Public Procurement Act, 2023.** The law on how government buys goods and works. It allows preferences for small, women-owned, youth-owned and Family Island businesses; the margin is set in each tender document.

**Pump (concrete pump).** A truck or trailer that pumps concrete through a hose to where it is needed, when the mixer truck cannot reach.

**Punch list (snag list).** The list of small items still to fix or finish at the end of the job, walked with the client and signed off item by item.
Example: "Master bath: re-caulk tub, touch up paint by door, adjust closet door." Your template: punch items finished within 10 working days.

**Purchase order (PO).** A written order you send a supplier: items, quantity, price, delivery date and address, payment terms, job and cost code.
Why it matters: it locks the price, records your committed cost, and settles arguments about what you ordered.

## R

**Rafter.** A sloping roof beam cut and built on site. Trusses are factory-made; rafters are built by the carpenter on site. Both need straps.

**Ready-mix.** Concrete batched at a plant and delivered in a mixer truck, with a delivery ticket showing the mix.

**Real property tax.** Annual tax on property you own. Applies to your rental assets later.

**Rebar (reinforcing bar).** Ribbed steel bars cast inside concrete to carry tension. Concrete is strong when squeezed but cracks when pulled; the steel takes the pull.

**Reconcile (bank reconciliation).** Matching every line on the bank statement with your own records each month, so your books match the bank.
Why it matters: it catches missed invoices, double payments and fraud.

**Registered agent.** A licensed firm that forms and maintains companies for clients and files with the Registrar General.

**Registrar General's Department.** The government office that registers business names and companies.

**Release agent (form oil).** Oil or coating brushed on formwork so concrete does not stick to it.

**Render.** See **stucco / render**.

**Retention.** A percentage held back from each payment until the work is accepted and the defects period ends. You hold 10% from subs. A client may hold 5% to 10% from you.
Example: sub invoice B$5,000. You pay B$4,500 and hold B$500. Half (B$250) released at practical completion, half at the end of the defects period if defects are fixed.

**RFI (request for information).** A written question to the designer or client when the drawings are unclear, missing something, or contradict each other. Numbered, dated, answered in writing.
Example: "RFI 03: Drawing S2 shows #5 bars in the tie beam; detail 4 shows #4. Which governs?" Never guess on structure.

**Right to suspend.** The contract clause that lets you stop work if the client does not pay. Your template: sum unpaid 7 days after due date, then 3 days' written notice.
Why it matters: with no lien law, stopping work is your main lever.

**Ring beam.** See **tie beam (belt course, ring beam)**.

**Ring-shank nail.** A nail with rings around the shank that grip the wood. Much harder to pull out than a smooth nail. Used for roof sheathing and strap connections where drawn.

**Rodding.** Pushing a steel rod up and down in fresh core fill or concrete to work out air pockets. A vibrator does the same job better.

**Rolling 12-month turnover.** Your total taxable sales for the last 12 months, recalculated every month.
Why it matters: VAT registration is mandatory once it passes B$100,000. Check it monthly. See **VAT registration threshold** for a worked example.

**Rough-in.** Installing the pipes, wires, boxes, ducts and drains inside walls, floors and ceilings before they are closed up. Inspected before it is covered.

## S

**Scaffold.** A temporary working platform. Must stand on a firm base, be braced, fully planked, with guardrails.

**Schedule A and Schedule B.** In your owner contract, Schedule A is the Scope of Works and Schedule B is the Exclusions. Both are part of the contract.

**Scope of work.** The written description of exactly what you will do, line by line, to what standard, tied to drawings.
Why it matters: scope you did not write down is scope you do for free.

**Selections.** The client's choices of finishes: tile, fixtures, paint colours, cabinets. Each has a deadline in the schedule. Late selections extend time, in writing.

**Self-perform.** Doing a trade with your own employed crew instead of a sub. Stage 2 in your roadmap. Captures the sub's margin and gives you control of the schedule.

**Septic tank.** An underground tank where household sewage settles and breaks down. The liquid flows on to a soak-away or drain field. Distances from wells are set by health rules.

**Setbacks.** The minimum distance a building must be from the property lines (front, sides, back), set by Town Planning.
Why it matters: a wall built 2 feet into the setback can mean refusal, a fight with the neighbour, or demolition.

**Setting out (set out).** Marking on the ground exactly where the building goes, using pegs, batter boards, string lines and levels, measured from the boundary pegs and the site plan.
Example check: a 30 x 40 ft rectangle has diagonals of exactly 50 ft (the 3-4-5 triangle). If the two diagonals differ, the building is not square.

**Sheathing.** Plywood or OSB sheets nailed over the trusses to form the roof deck. Nailing pattern at the edges and in the field (the middle of the sheet) per the drawing.

**Shingles / square.** Asphalt shingles are a common roofing. Roofing is measured in "squares": 1 square = 100 sq ft.
Example: 1,800 sq ft of roof / 100 = 18 squares, + 12% for hips, valleys and waste = 20.2. Order 21 squares.

**Shoring.** Temporary support: props under formwork and beams, or supports holding back trench sides.
Why it matters: trenches over 5 feet deep must be shored or sloped. Nobody goes in an unsupported trench.

**Shop drawing.** A detailed drawing made by a supplier or sub (truss layout, window schedule, cabinet layout) showing exactly how they will make or fit their product. Submitted for approval before fabrication.

**Site plan.** A drawing of the lot showing the building position, setbacks, drive and septic. Your own site plan adds deliveries, storage, skip bin, toilet, parking and security.

**Skip bin.** A large steel container dropped on site for debris, then collected and emptied by the hire company.
Why it matters: skip hire and dump fees are general conditions. Price them.

**Slab (slab on grade).** The concrete floor poured on compacted fill inside the stem walls.

**Sleeve.** A piece of pipe set through a slab, beam or wall before the pour so a pipe or cable can pass through later without cutting concrete.

**Slope factor.** A multiplier that turns a roof's plan (flat, as seen from above) area into its real sloped area. Steeper roofs have bigger factors.
Example: plan area 1,500 sq ft x 1.2 (about an 8 in 12 pitch) = 1,800 sq ft of roof. Get the factor for your pitch from a table or the drawings.

**Slump.** A measure of how wet and runny fresh concrete is. A cone is filled with concrete and lifted off; how far the pile sinks, in inches, is the slump. The specified slump is on the drawings or the mix order.
Why it matters: drivers and crews add water to raise the slump and make concrete easier to place. Extra water weakens it. No water added without your OK.

**Snag list.** See **punch list**.

**Soak-away.** A pit filled with rock, or a perforated chamber, that lets water soak into the ground: septic effluent or roof and yard drainage. Distances from wells per health rules.

**Soil treatment (termite treatment).** A termite poison applied to the soil before the slab is poured, by a pest control company. Get a written record of what was applied and when.

**Sole trader.** You trading in your own name or a registered business name. You and the business are the same legal person, so business debts and claims are yours personally.

**Spalling.** Concrete breaking off in flakes or chunks because the rebar inside rusted and swelled. Caused by low cover, beach sand, or cracks letting salt water in.

**Specifications (specs).** The written part of the design: materials, products, standards, workmanship, testing. Drawings show where and how big; specs say what and how good.

**Stamp duty.** A tax on property transfers and some documents.

**Stamped drawings.** Drawings sealed and signed by a qualified architect or engineer who takes professional responsibility for the design.
Why it matters: structural sizes, rebar, beams, footings and connections come from stamped drawings. Never from you, your sub or a guess.

**Stem wall.** The block wall from the footing up to slab level.

**Stirrup.** A loop of smaller bar wrapped around the main bars of a beam (like the tie beam) at regular spacing. Holds the cage together and resists shear (the force trying to slice the beam).

**String line.** Tight string between batter boards or pegs marking wall lines and heights.

**Stucco / render.** A cement and sand plaster coat on block walls. Outside it is usually called stucco or render; inside, plaster. Applied in coats.
Why it matters: it is the first weather skin on a block wall. Cracked or thin stucco lets water in.

**Sub (subcontractor).** A separate business you hire to do part of the work for a price. They work under your subcontract, and you are responsible to the client for their work.

**Submittal.** Product information, shop drawings or samples you send to the designer or client to approve before ordering or installing.
Example: window submittal: product approval, sizes, glass, frame colour, anchoring. Approved in writing before you order. Nobody can say "that's not what I wanted" when it lands 12 weeks later.

**Superintendent (super).** The person running the site day to day: safety, quality, schedule, subs, daily records. Priorities every day: safety, quality, schedule, cost. At the start you are both PM and super.

**Supplier registry.** The government's list of registered suppliers and contractors, joined through the eProcurement portal.

**Surety.** An insurance company that issues bonds (bid, performance, advance payment) for a contractor.

**Survey / surveyor.** A licensed land surveyor measures and marks the property boundaries and produces a survey plan.
Why it matters: for new work, get one. Setbacks are measured from the true boundary, not from the old fence.

## T

**Takeoff (quantity takeoff).** Measuring quantities from the drawings or the site: cubic yards of concrete, number of blocks, square feet of tile. Show the math so it can be checked.

**Tariff Act.** The law whose First Schedule sets the customs duty rate for each item.

**Tax account.** A separate bank account where you move VAT collected, NIB and a business licence reserve every time you are paid. That money is not yours.

**Tax Compliance Certificate.** A document showing your business is up to date with its taxes. Government tenders typically ask for one, alongside your business licence and NIB letter. Ask the Department of Inland Revenue how to get it and what must be current.

**Termination.** Ending the contract early. Your template: allowed for a material breach not fixed within 10 days of written notice. It also says what gets paid on termination.

**Termites.** Insects that eat wood. A constant threat here. The answers: soil treatment before the slab, treated lumber where specified, no wood touching soil.

**Testimonial.** A short written statement from a satisfied client. For your marketing and licensing file.

**Thinset.** Cement-based adhesive mortar used to stick tile.

**Three-week look-ahead.** See **look-ahead schedule**.

**Tie beam (belt course, ring beam).** Three names for the same thing: the reinforced concrete beam cast on top of the block walls, running all the way round the building. It ties the walls together and holds the hurricane straps that anchor the roof.
Why it matters: it is the link between walls and roof. Straps must be set in it before the pour, at every truss position.

**Time and materials (T&M).** A contract where the client pays your labour at agreed rates plus materials at cost plus markup. Use it with a cap: "not to exceed B$X without written approval".
Example: labour B$45 an hour x 16 hours = B$720; materials B$400 + 20% = B$480; total B$1,200, under a B$1,500 cap. Good for repairs where you cannot see the problem until you open it up.

**Title retention (title to materials).** A contract clause saying materials delivered to site stay your property until paid for, so you can take back what is not yet built in if the client does not pay.

**Toolbox talk.** A 5-minute safety talk at the start of the day on one topic: ladders, heat, cutting, trenches. Write the topic in the daily report.

**Town Planning / Building Control.** Two separate approvals. Town Planning (Department of Physical Planning) decides whether you may build that use, that size, in that place: zoning, use, setbacks, subdivision. Building Control (Ministry of Public Works) decides whether the design and construction are safe and to code: plan review, permit, stage inspections, certificate of occupancy.

**Trade account.** A credit account with a supplier: you take materials now and pay by the agreed date, often 30 days.
Why it matters: pay on time and it is free financing. Pay late and you lose it, and your name with it.

**Trade credit.** The free financing you get from trade accounts.

**Trade licence.** A licence a tradesperson needs to practise a regulated trade, mainly electricians and plumbers.

**Truss.** A factory-made triangle frame of lumber joined with metal plates, designed by the truss maker's engineer. A row of trusses forms the roof frame. Lead time 2 to 6 weeks.

**Turnover.** See **gross turnover**.

## U

**Underlayment.** A waterproof layer (felt or synthetic) over the roof sheathing, under the shingles or metal. Laps run so water flows over each joint, not under it.

**Unforeseen conditions.** Things you could not reasonably see or expect when you priced: rot, termite damage, mould, corroded steel, bad existing wiring or plumbing, rock, buried debris or services. Your contract prices them as a change.
Example: opening a bathroom wall reveals a termite-eaten wall plate. Stop, photograph, notify the client, agree a change order (say B$850), then fix it.

**Unit price.** A contract or rate priced per unit of work and paid on the measured quantity. You carry the productivity risk.
Example (illustration): block laying at an agreed B$3.00 per block. 2,400 blocks measured = B$7,200. Your real rate comes from your sub quotes.

**Utility connections.** Hooking the building up to BPL power, Water & Sewerage water and sewer, and cable. Done after the certificate of occupancy and sign-offs. Fees normally excluded from your price.

---

## V

**Validity date.** How long your quote's price stands, often 30 days. It must match your suppliers' quote validity, or you need an escalation clause.

**Vapour barrier.** A sheet of heavy plastic (polyethylene) laid under the slab over the compacted fill, thickness per the drawings or spec, laps overlapped and taped. It stops ground moisture rising through the slab into floors and finishes.
Why it matters: a torn or missing barrier means damp floors, lifting tile and mould.

**Variation.** The UK and JCT word for a change order. Your subcontract uses "variation" for changes to a sub's work.

**VAT (value added tax).** A 10% tax on most goods and services (cut from 12% in 2022). Construction services are standard-rated. Confirm with the Department of Inland Revenue.

**VAT registration threshold.** Registration is mandatory once your taxable supplies pass B$100,000 in any 12 months. You can register voluntarily below that.
Example: your 12 months to end of January total B$92,000. In February you invoice B$15,000. Last February you invoiced B$5,000, which drops out of the window. New rolling total: 92,000 - 5,000 + 15,000 = B$102,000. You have passed B$100,000: register. Ask Inland Revenue how quickly you must register once you cross.

**Vibrator (concrete vibrator, poker).** A tool with a vibrating head pushed into wet concrete to shake out air so it fills around the bars and into the corners.
Why it matters: unvibrated concrete has voids (honeycombing) that weaken it and expose the steel.

---

## W

**Waiver of claims.** A clause saying you lose your right to claim time or money if you do not give notice within a short period, sometimes 2 or 3 days.
Why it matters: red flag. Negotiate it to a workable period.

**Warranty.** Your promise to fix defects in your work for a set period. Your template: 12 months on workmanship from practical completion. Manufacturer warranties on products pass to the client. Excludes misuse, storms, work by others, and owner-supplied items.

**Washed sand.** Sand washed to remove salt and clay. The only sand for concrete, mortar and stucco. Never beach sand.

**Waste factor.** An extra percentage added to quantities for cutting, breakage and spillage. Examples: block 5%, concrete 5% to 10%, tile 10% to 15%, drywall 10%.

**Water & Sewerage.** The Water and Sewerage Corporation, the public water and sewer utility. Connection after final sign-offs.

**Wharfage.** A port charge on goods landed at the dock. Part of landed cost.

**WIP (work in progress).** Jobs started but not finished. A WIP report compares, for each job, what you have earned (by percent complete) with what you have billed.
Example: B$40,000 job, estimated total cost B$31,000, cost to date B$20,000. Percent complete = 20,000 / 31,000 = 64.5%. Earned = 64.5% x 40,000 = B$25,800.

| Billed to date | Result | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| B$26,000 | On track (B$200 ahead) | Fine |
| B$30,000 | Overbilled B$4,200 | You owe that much work. Do not spend that cash as profit. |
| B$20,000 | Underbilled B$5,800 | You are financing the client. Invoice now. |

**Win rate.** Quotes won divided by quotes sent. 25% to 40% is healthy. Much higher means you are too cheap. Much lower means too high, or the wrong clients.

**Work permit.** Permission from the Department of Immigration for a non-Bahamian to work. Employing someone without permission is an offence. Check your own employees, require subs to certify their crews, and spot-check.

**Working days.** Days work normally happens. Define it in the contract (your template's site hours are Monday to Saturday). "20 working days" on a six-day week is a little over three calendar weeks.

**Workmanlike (good and workmanlike manner).** The legal standard that work is done with the skill and care of a competent tradesperson. It is in your owner contract.
