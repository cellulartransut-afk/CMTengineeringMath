# 06. Money: pricing, cash flow, banking, insurance, bonds, wealth

As of 2026-11-03.

## First principles

- **Revenue** is what the client agrees to pay.
- **Direct cost** is what the job costs: materials, subs, labour, equipment, general conditions.
- **Gross profit** = revenue minus direct cost. It pays your overhead and your profit.
- **Overhead** is what the business costs whether or not you have a job: phone, vehicle, insurance, licences, software, accounting, marketing, your office, your own salary.
- **Net profit** = gross profit minus overhead. This is what grows the company.
- **Cash** is what is in the bank today. A profitable company with no cash dies. A company with cash survives mistakes.

## Markup is not margin

Markup is added to cost. Margin is the share of the price you keep. Contractors who mix them up underprice every job.

| Markup on cost | Gross margin on price |
|---|---|
| 10% | 9.1% |
| 15% | 13.0% |
| 20% | 16.7% |
| 25% | 20.0% |
| 30% | 23.1% |
| 40% | 28.6% |
| 50% | 33.3% |

```
Margin = Markup / (1 + Markup)
Markup = Margin / (1 - Margin)
Price  = Cost / (1 - target margin)
```

To keep a 20% margin you must mark up 25%, not 20%.

## Setting your markup

1. Add up your yearly overhead, including a fair salary for yourself.
2. Estimate your yearly revenue.
3. Overhead % = overhead / revenue.
4. Target margin = overhead % + profit % (net profit target, 8% to 10% for small work).

Example: overhead B$40,000 on revenue B$250,000 = 16%. Add 8% profit. Target margin 24%. Markup = 0.24 / 0.76 = 31.6%.

**Small jobs need higher margins.** A B$1,500 repair takes nearly as much of your time to sell, schedule and manage as a B$8,000 one. Set a minimum job charge (for example B$350 to B$500) and use 30% to 40% margin on small jobs. Larger, simpler jobs can take less.

**Break-even revenue** = monthly overhead / gross margin %. With B$2,000 overhead and 22% margin you need B$9,091 of revenue every month just to stand still.

## Cash flow on a job

Example: B$40,000 renovation, 10 weeks.

| Milestone | Client pays | Cumulative in | Cumulative spent | Cash position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signing (deposit 40%) | 16,000 | 16,000 | 12,000 (materials, mobilisation) | +4,000 |
| Demolition and rough-in done (25%) | 10,000 | 26,000 | 20,000 | +6,000 |
| Finishes installed (25%) | 10,000 | 36,000 | 29,000 | +7,000 |
| Completion and punch list (10%) | 4,000 | 40,000 | 31,000 | +9,000 |

The rule: at every milestone the cash position must stay **positive**. If your payment schedule makes it go negative, you are financing the client. Change the schedule before you sign.

Use the **Cash Flow** sheet in `consultant/tools/estimator.xlsx` to test every job.

## Books and banking

- **Business bank account from day one.** Every business cent in and out goes through it. Never mix personal and business money.
- **Accounting software** (QuickBooks, Xero, Wave or similar). Record every invoice and receipt the week it happens.
- **Job costing.** Every cost coded to a job and a cost code (for example 03 Concrete, 04 Masonry, 06 Carpentry, 09 Finishes, 15 Plumbing, 16 Electrical, 01 General conditions).
- **Separate tax account.** Move VAT collected, NIB and a business licence reserve into it every time you are paid. That money is not yours.
- **Monthly close:** cash in bank, money owed to you, money you owe, profit per job, overhead.
- **Accountant:** once a year at least, to prepare financial statements. By year 2 or 3 get reviewed statements; banks and bond providers will ask.

## Credit and financing

- Pay suppliers on time to build trade credit (30-day accounts). That is free financing.
- Build bank history: business account, small facility, repay early.
- Access Accelerator and Bahamas Development Bank programmes (see `02-bahamas-rules.md`) for equipment and working capital.
- Borrow only for things that earn their payments (a truck that lets you take more work, a mixer you'd otherwise rent every week). Never borrow to cover a losing job; fix the pricing instead.

## Reserves

- Overhead reserve: 3 months of overhead in cash before you grow.
- Hurricane season reserve: extra cash going into June for delays and damage.
- Tax account: always full.

## Insurance

| Cover | What it does | When |
|---|---|---|
| Public liability (general liability) | Damage or injury to third parties from your work | Before your first job |
| Contractor's all risks (CAR) | Damage to the works during construction: fire, theft, storm | Each job, or an annual policy; check named-storm cover |
| Motor | Your vehicles | Always |
| Tools and equipment | Theft and damage | When the kit is worth it |
| Employees | NIB industrial benefits cover work injuries for employees who are contributed for | When you hire |
| Professional indemnity | Design errors | Only if you do design-build |

Get quotes from at least two Bahamian brokers. Require every sub to carry public liability and to name you on the certificate.

## Bonds and guarantees

Big clients and government may ask for:
- **Bid bond:** proves you'll sign if you win.
- **Performance bond:** often 10% of the contract value; pays the client if you fail to finish.
- **Advance payment guarantee:** protects the client's deposit.

In The Bahamas these come from banks (bank guarantees, often against cash collateral or a credit facility) or insurers (surety). They want financial statements, a track record and collateral. Start building that record from your first year.

## Paying yourself

- Fixed monthly salary set in your overhead. Don't drain the account when a big cheque lands.
- Profit stays in the company until reserves are full.
- After reserves: split profit between growth (equipment, people), assets (Stage 3), and you.

## Numbers to watch every month

| Number | Healthy for a small contractor |
|---|---|
| Gross margin per job vs estimate | Within 2 to 3 points of the estimate |
| Overhead as % of revenue | Falling as you grow |
| Net profit | 8%+ |
| Days to get paid (receivables) | Under 30 days private, track government separately |
| Backlog (signed work not yet done) | 2 to 3 months of work |
| Win rate on quotes | 25% to 40%; much higher means you're too cheap, much lower means too high or wrong clients |
| Callbacks | Falling |
| Safety incidents | Zero |

## Turning contracting profit into assets

**Rental unit test (example, illustrative numbers):**

| Line | B$ |
|---|---|
| Build cost at your cost (no markup), one 2-bed unit | 180,000 |
| Land share | 40,000 |
| Total invested | 220,000 |
| Rent per month | 1,800 |
| Yearly rent | 21,600 |
| Less vacancy, maintenance, insurance, property tax, management (about 30%) | -6,480 |
| Net yearly income | 15,120 |
| **Net yield** = 15,120 / 220,000 | **6.9%** |

Compare the yield with the bank's loan rate. If the yield is above your borrowing cost, the asset pays for its own loan and then pays you. Your edge as the builder is building at cost, which no ordinary buyer can do.

**Equipment test:** payback months = purchase price / (monthly rental income minus monthly running, maintenance and insurance). Under 24 months with steady demand is a good buy.
